The purpose of this paper is to discover whether there are societal, economic, and cultural differences in Zoroastrian society in Iran versus India. The argument this paper constructs is two-fold: firstly, that Zoroastrian society pre-conquest was susceptible to a divide because of the over-integration of the religion with the location of its followers, and secondly, that as a result, a divide has manifested in the categories listed above. The key findings of the paper can be found in the second section, where the differences are extrapolated from current information on the two societies.

*Conflicts of interest: the author has declared that they themselves are a member of the Parsee community discussed in this paper, which could subconsciously have led to bias in the paper.*

## Introduction

After threats of persecution and mass migration, the Zoroastrian religion is now showing declining population numbers and division within the community over the last few decades. This emerging threat begs the question: what are some of the societal, economic, and cultural differences that are prevalent in the modern day as a result of the divide created by the Arab conquest of Persia?

Before analyzing how to answer this question, we must first set the context. The Zoroastrian religion started in ancient Persia (modern-day Iran) around 1500 BCE, following the teachings of the prophet Zarathustra. The Zoroastrians, the prevalent ethno-religious group in Persia, lived through the Achaemenid, Parthian, and Sasanian empires, and were the first people to believe in the idea of dualism — the fight of good versus evil — an idea that has manifested itself at the core of many later religions, such as Christianity as well as Hinduism. Following the Sasanian empire, the Arab conquest of Persia in 651 CE left the religion divided into three main factions: those who fled Persia to the nearby port of Gujarat, where they were welcomed and partly assimilated into the culture; those who stayed and avoided persecution under Islamic rule inside Persia, and still reside in Iran; and those who stayed and avoided Islamic persecution but later migrated to India in the 19th century.

This paper will analyze the cultural, economic, and social differences of the first two factions — those who originally fled to India and those who stayed and still reside within Iran — through analysing the socio-economic divide between the faction that migrated to India (commonly referred to as Parsees/Parsis) versus the faction that still resides in Iran (Iranian Zoroastrians), as well as primary sourced data on the economic prosperity of both factions. This paper aims to fill a gap in research on the current topic: whilst many have theorized as to why Zoroastrians are successful in certain regions, and have analyzed their ancient cultural background, seldom has it been analyzed how a gap has been created in the religion as a result of the annexation of Persia. The criteria for evaluative analysis of both parties in this paper are economic prosperity, cultural adaption, and social harmony and acceptance within their respective communities.

## Section 1: Understanding Zoroastrianism

Analysis of data on the social, economic, and cultural standing of Zoroastrians in Iran and India after the Arab conquest of Persia in 651 CE denotes a significant divide between the Parsis in India and the Iranian Zoroastrians. This immediate divergence is representative of much larger, long-term differences between the two societies and the impact that has had on the religion. Understanding the Zoroastrian faith and its theological guidelines, as well as the factors that made it susceptible to divide, is important groundwork: this paper will first analyze the distinct history of the two parties, then deconstruct the differences that became prevalent after the Arab invasion but were non-existent before it, aiming to prove that the way Zoroastrianism was manifested in Persia made it susceptible to division post-annexation.

The essential core belief of Zoroastrianism is that there is an omnipotent god named Ahura Mazda who spreads the word of the Asha (eternal/cosmic law). Ahura Mazda, while omnipotent, relies on humans to make three decisions in their everyday lives to keep the evil spirit of the world (Ahriman) at bay: "Good thoughts, Good words, Good deeds" (Humata, Huxta, Huvarshta). Following these three principles, along with conducting the ceremonial aspects of the religion, makes one a good human and a driver of the force of good in the eyes of Ahura Mazda. One key difference in the religious celebratory aspect of the two parties is that Iranian Zoroastrians celebrate Nowruz (New Year by the ancient Iranian calendar) by laying out a table called the Haft-Sin table on the 21st of March — a tradition that is a staple of Iranian Zoroastrian culture but is practically non-existent among the Parsis. This can be seen as an interlinkage between one's Zoroastrian and Iranian identity that has cemented over centuries of residence in Iran, while Parsis today feel a much weaker connection to the region because of centuries of absence — compounded by a reluctance to associate with a region now perceived as Islamic, and one whose rulers historically oppressed and persecuted Zoroastrians.

One of the key texts in the Zoroastrian religion is the Zend Avesta (translation: "interpretation of the Avesta"), the Zoroastrian scripture parallel to the Christian Bible or Islamic Quran, compiled during the Sasanian Empire across all of ancient Persia, when Zoroastrianism was by far the dominant religion. Under the Avesta, two crucial sections stand out: the Yasna, the name for the religious prayer performed daily in fire temples, and the Vendidad, the moral code of the Zoroastrians which served as the basis for the legal code of the Sasanian empire. These two fundamental texts have stayed the same since the Arab occupation of Persia, and are practiced and interpreted the same way by both parties — a key similarity between Parsis and Iranian Zoroastrians. It can be analyzed here that when a part of the religion isolates itself from its surroundings and strictly focuses on the theological aspect, such as the Avesta, it manages to stay practiced across both cultures — suggesting that it is the integration of religion with geographical location that leads to division, not the theology itself.

The basic religious rituals of the Zoroastrian faith are the Navjote, the Yasna, and the Dakhma. The Yasna is the fire ceremony conducted in fire temples, where scripture is recited while facing a fire, a sign of purity. The Navjote is the initiation ceremony marking entry into the religion, conducted for a child between the ages of 7 and 12, presenting them with the religious clothing (sudreh and kusti) they will wear for the rest of their lives. The Dakhma is the ritual through which a member of the community is honoured at their passing: the deceased is left in a "Tower of Silence" atop a hill, where vultures pick at the body — considered the final act of charity a Zoroastrian can perform. While these ceremonies stay the same within both groups, it is worth noting the minute differences that hint at the division between them: the Tower of Silence in Yazd, Iran, is 15 kilometres from civilization, in the middle of the desert, while the Tower of Silence in Mumbai, India, sits in a relatively prominent location in the middle of the city — suggesting the Parsis have more resources and are more socially integrated than the Iranian Zoroastrians, whose religious structures remain isolated from public view.

Before analyzing the relationship between Zoroastrianism and Islam within Persia leading up to the annexation, it is worth understanding the influence Zoroastrianism had on the creation of Islam and the Abrahamic faiths more broadly — which further clarifies why exactly Zoroastrianism was susceptible to division after annexation. Historians identify a correlation between Zoroastrianism and the main Abrahamic faiths through the idea of dualism: Ahura Mazda, the omnipotent god, created two twins, Spenta Mainyu (force of good) and Angra Mainyu/Ahriman (force of evil), whose battle for influence over the world is the Zoroastrian explanation for all worldly events. This general idea of good versus evil is portrayed in all Abrahamic faiths, and even scriptural references are extremely similar — both Zoroastrianism and the Abrahamic faiths mention the battle of light versus darkness, and chapters 40–48 of the Book of Isaiah bear extreme resemblance to the third and fourth verses of Gatha 44. This further supports the idea that Zoroastrianism's susceptibility to division was no fault of theological ideology, but instead of over-integration with the region.

It is important to note that Islam was the only other prominent religion near Persia at the time, so it is speculated the Arabs may have been competing for land, converts, or resources — it was part of Islamic scripture to actively spread the faith, contrary to Zoroastrian scripture, where conversion was frowned upon as a symbol of impurity, which academic consensus holds as one of the largest motives for invasion. Despite the hostility of the invasion itself, there were semblances of prior interaction between the two peoples, such as trade conducted by Sassanian Zoroastrians with Arabic tribes through routes in the Persian Gulf — theorized as the means by which Zoroastrianism influenced Islam, with traders introducing ideas of dualism and the purity of fire. We can see a significant overlap between the Islamic Mahdi ("savior") and the Zoroastrian Saoshyant ("bringer of light") — both a savior who appears at the end of time to restore humanity and purify the world — as well as the shared significance of fire and light as symbols of purity, echoed in the Quran's description of Allah as the "Light of the heavens and the earth" (Surah 24:35).

Sections of the Bible — Daniel 1:21, 6:28, and 10:1; Ezra 1:1 and 6:14; and Isaiah 44:24–28 and 45:1 — all speak positively of Zoroastrian influence on biblical religion, though this may carry some bias, given the biblical population's interest in claiming interaction with and land in the Persian region; the lack of Zoroastrian records from the Achaemenid period, and of early Biblical and Islamic documentation, also limits large-scale analysis of the true extent of this influence. Effectively, what direct analysis of religious scripture shows is that there are noticeable Zoroastrian theological overlaps with the Abrahamic faiths, likely from trade relations in the region — meaning it was not the theological aspect of the religion that led to division, and cannot be considered the main reason for Zoroastrianism's susceptibility to it.

The earliest point at which we can date Zoroastrianism as dominant in Persia is the Achaemenid Empire. Under Cyrus the Great and then Darius the Great, the Zoroastrian religion was institutionalized within Persia — Zoroastrian law, for instance, became the law under which all of Persia operated. While there is debate over whether Cyrus the Great was Zoroastrian, the Persian population was very likely predominantly Zoroastrian during his rule: the gap between his rule and that of Darius the Great, who was demonstrably Zoroastrian and revered Ahura Mazda, was only about ten years, and given the lack of state-wide communication infrastructure at the time, it is extremely unlikely that word of mouth alone could have spread Zoroastrianism across the whole Persian empire in that window if the population was not already largely Zoroastrian under Cyrus.

The Behistun inscriptions in Iran, dated to the Sasanian empire and specifically the rule of Darius the Great, depict a group of people around a spiritual figure in the air — the symbol of Ahura Mazda, the same symbol used repeatedly in later scriptures such as the Zend Avesta. Academic consensus holds that the figures being held with their hands behind their backs are people who have committed a crime, with Darius the Great holding them for judgment beneath Ahura Mazda. If the rhetoric spread through such inscriptions was that one would be judged according to Zoroastrian principles, it is quite likely the population was itself Zoroastrian — otherwise, forcing them to live by an unfamiliar religion's principles would likely have provoked revolt and discontent, of which there is no evidence. Following Darius's rule, Persian identity became centered around Zoroastrian principles such as fire worship and the Magi priesthood, further supporting the idea that this over-unification of Zoroastrianism with Persian culture was the reason for the later divide.

Moving into the Parthian empire, Zoroastrian identity remained strongly linked with Persian identity — more fire temples were built and the recitation of the Yasna became an everyday practice, and even amid Greek influence in the region during this period, the religion remained relatively resilient. The early Sasanian empire can be seen as the peak of the Zoroastrian religion: the Magi priesthood was common, fire temples were frequently built, and Zoroastrianism became the official state-sanctioned religion — reconfirming Persian identity's infusion with Zoroastrianism. The issue with this uniform identity, however, was that every person held a different position on the spectrum of self-association between their Persian and Zoroastrian identities, and this semi-forceful unification led to an unresolved decision calculus once the choice to leave or stay finally had to be made.

Come 651 CE, we arrive at the annexation of Persia by the Arabs, led by Caliph Umar. Notable battles had already been lost by the Persians, such as the Battle of Nahavand in 642 CE, leading to the moment King Yazdegerd III, leader of the Sasanian empire, was murdered, marking the end of the war and an Arab victory. After the occupation, the Umayyads (the first Arab dynasty) wasted no time conducting rapid Islamization of the Persian people. Within the Persian populace, some converted out of fear for their lives, while others attempted to maintain their religious identity — and did so in different ways. Through the Qissa-i-Sanjan, we know large groups fled to Sanjan, Gujarat, in India: these were the Parsis. Others felt conflicted enough about leaving Persia that they stayed and developed enclaves such as Yazd, where the largest Zoroastrian fire temple in Iran is located today.

Through this analysis of the unity of Zoroastrian identity followed by the near-instant schism of the religion, it is clear that the occupation of Persia was the driving factor behind the longstanding division between the two groups today — and that the root cause of the religion's susceptibility to division was the lack of a unified decision-making calculus among the populace. The second section of this paper moves to analyze the direct impacts of that division as it has permeated physically — cultural division, economic disparity, and societal acceptance.

## The Divergence

Decisions have consequences. The ancestors of today's Iranian Zoroastrians chose to prioritize loyalty to their state by neither migrating away nor converting to Islam; those choices have practical effects on their descendants to this day. Those who chose to flee their birthplace for India out of fear of religious persecution or forced conversion made a different decision, with equally real consequences for their own descendants. This section explicitly analyzes and decomposes what those subtle but also blatant consequences have been for each community — first societal differences, then economic differences, and finally cultural differences.

### Societal Differences

After the Arab invasion in 651 CE, the Islamic rulers instituted the jizya tax — a policy first used in Persia that later became standard across all Islamic dominions — under which any non-Muslims living in an Islamic state paid heavy taxes to the government in return for protection. In theory this offered a path to religious freedom within the Islamic state; in practice, minute government acknowledgement of the right to practice did not translate into much. The Islamic historian Muhammad Al-Tabari described the forms of social marginalization taking place within Persia under Islamic rule, writing that non-Muslim people were not allowed to practice religion publicly and were barred from certain roles in society. For the Iranian Zoroastrians, life was one not of religious freedom and autonomy, but of domineering oppression.

During this period, the Parsees who had migrated to India had already begun significantly adapting to their new region, leaving behind traditions close to their hearts from Persia. The Parsees began using Gujarati instead of continuing to speak Farsi — a clear act of cultural assimilation that was not an option for the Iranians. This assimilation had a limit, however: the king of Gujarat at the time stated that Parsees were free to stay as long as they followed three rules — Parsee women would only marry Parsee men and vice versa; Parsees had to speak the local language; and Parsees had to dress like the locals, meaning saris for women, with no clear guideline for men. The two cultures blended well; today, Parsee women often wear the "Gara" — a modified version of the sari — when dressing traditionally.

In Iran, the Iranian Zoroastrians had no choice but to form ethnic enclaves, mainly in Yazd and Kerman, to avoid high taxation and garner some bare level of religious freedom. Without these areas, it is unlikely the religion would have survived to the modern day, as constant persecution and harassment would inevitably have led to its erosion. To be a practicing member of Zoroastrian society in Iran, one effectively had to stay within a religious enclave.

Comparatively, in India, the Parsees faced a somewhat similar situation, where the laws set by the Gujarat king forced them to isolate themselves from the rest of society. Parsees ended up creating their own educational systems and ecosystems in which to thrive. There are still lasting impacts of this isolation: even though Parsees are far more integrated within India today, they often still live in Parsee colonies where children play together and speak Gujarati, and adults typically spend their evenings socializing mainly with other Parsees — a pattern often credited as one of the main reasons there hasn't been greater cultural erosion within the community.

Another pivotal aspect of a religious society is the freedom to practice openly. In Iran, Islamic rule destroyed the majority of fire temples, making it near impossible to practice daily rituals such as the Yasna — this, combined with the fear of being exposed as Zoroastrian, drove the religion into private practice, with families conducting prayers inside their own homes, sometimes developing quite different rituals from other families in the same area. Therefore, despite living in close-knit religious enclaves, the Iranian Zoroastrians' interpretation and practice of the religion became fragmented, since it could only be conducted behind closed doors.

Comparatively, the Parsees in India were more unified in religious practice. Though segregated from the rest of society, they were not forbidden from practising their religion — fire temples were established where people could pray together, and religious bodies such as Panchayats were established to set rules around religious practice. A prominent example is the Atash Berams (fire temples) developed in the early years of Parsee residence in India, the most prominent being the Iranshah, a large fire temple in Udvada, Gujarat. This opportunity for open, public practice supports the premise that the Parsees were, and remain, more socially unified.

From a statistical standpoint, the Zoroastrian population in Iran is a mere 25,000, per the 2016 Iranian census, most densely concentrated in urban areas such as Yazd, Tehran, and Kerman — a steep drop from the population directly after the Arab conquest. It is worth questioning this figure's validity: while it is unlikely the raw census data was tampered with, there is a high potential that practicing Zoroastrians were afraid to admit their faith for fear of discrimination — evidenced by the fact that Zoroastrians are, by law, not permitted to hold positions in the Iranian government, suggesting religious diversity is not a value reflected in Iranian law. This discrimination is likely a driver of the reported population decline, as more people move away from openly identifying with the religion.

Analogously, in India, per the 2011 census, the population of Zoroastrians is 60,000, also densely concentrated in one city, Mumbai — reflecting a decline that, while present, is not as steep as in Iran. Here it cannot be attributed to discrimination, since Parsees are widely accepted: the Prime Minister of India, Narendra Modi, has publicly referenced the Parsees in speeches, even wishing them a happy Nowruz. The reasons behind India's population decline are instead multi-faceted: the Parsee population holds uniquely conservative views relative to Zoroastrians globally, alienating certain groups from joining the religion in several ways — for example, children can only join the religion if their father is Parsee, not their mother; LGBTQ people are ostracized; and conversion is deeply forbidden. This, combined with a move away from the endogamy the religion demands, has resulted in a steady population decline for the Parsees over the years.

To synthesize: from a distance, there are many similarities between the Parsees of India and the Iranian Zoroastrians — both have formed enclaves, and both have declining populations — but closer examination reveals stark differences, such as the oppressive state under which Iranian Zoroastrians live compared to the accepting integration of Parsees in India, or the conservative views of the Parsees against the comparatively more accepting norms among Iranians. The environments these two groups inhabit are vastly different, yet have shaped very different everyday practices and values in each.

### Economic Differences

To begin this section: post-Arab conquest, an immediate jizya tax was instituted on all legal non-Muslim groups in Persia. In theory, paying this tax secured Zoroastrians the freedom to practice their religion within the Islamic state; in reality, minute government acknowledgement did little for them in practice. The nationalist and patriotic character of the Islamic population meant many people, having lost husbands, brothers, or sons in the recent Arab conquest itself, were reluctant to accept the religion of an army that had killed their family members — regardless of what the government formally permitted. Beyond that resentment, the nature of Islamic scripture, which emphasizes spreading the faith as a principle rewarded in the afterlife, also made it hard for Zoroastrianism to be accepted and made active conversion efforts common, in turn making it harder for Zoroastrians to be open about their faith. The jizya tax therefore had direct economic harms — lowering average Zoroastrian income and disposable income, and making it harder to save and build generational wealth — without delivering the religious freedoms it promised. This discrimination persists today at the legislative level: Articles 64, 67, 91, and 115 of the Iranian Constitution outline representation in parliament in terms that make it impossible for a non-Muslim to hold a position, meaning Zoroastrians in Iran remain unrepresented in decision-making that directly affects them economically — with no path to argue for affirmative action or tax relief as a religious minority.

This continuous discrimination pushed Iranian Zoroastrians into rural jobs such as farming, avoiding discrimination in the big cities, often within Zoroastrian enclaves such as Yazd or Kerman — locking them into a cycle of poverty likely to continue for generations, given how uncompetitive agriculture is as an industry in Iran.

Comparatively, the Parsees' economic history, from their arrival in Sanjan to the present day, looks very different. Upon arrival, the Parsees quickly began trade initiatives along the Silk Road, trading spices and textiles from China to the Middle East — evidence that, whatever resentment they carried from the Arab conquest, the Parsees prioritized economic prosperity, continuing to trade with Arabic peoples for centuries. Their geographic location at the port of Sanjan, a crucial trade harbour, significantly aided this development. From arrival until around the 17th century, most Parsees worked as merchants and traders, with notable numbers in the textile industry; after the 17th century, significant numbers moved into rudimentary banking and independent moneylending. The next major period was British occupation, from the 18th to the 20th century, as the British occupied Bombay (now Mumbai) — again, geography worked in the Parsees' favour, as Mumbai served as a trading hub for goods like cotton and opium that attracted British settlement. Already established in Mumbai, the Parsees quickly picked up English and began working for British companies such as the East India Company, and, using the skills learned there, transitioned into jobs such as banking, business, law, and — famously — medicine: at one point in the 1980s, 80% of doctors in Mumbai were Parsees, a remarkable feat for a group of only around 100,000 people in a city whose population exceeded 12 million.

Today the Parsees are, per capita, the richest ethnic group in India, and continuously participate in philanthropy and investment in their religion, funding buildings such as fire temples. The Tata Group, founded by Jamshedji Tata — the largest conglomerate in India, with a market cap of over 400 billion US dollars — sits at the heart of Parsee philanthropy, funding scholarships and charity work for young Parsees, and has become a symbol of Parsee status in society: the current leader of the Tata Group is arguably the most influential Parsee in India at any given time. This has created a stereotype that Parsees are smart, well-spoken, and good with money — a stereotype that continues to generate real, positive results, through a subconscious bias associating "being Parsee" with money and opportunity, and in turn better jobs and social status.

Comparing the two groups directly: the first piece of analysis is geography. While the Parsees were located near the sea and a port, the Iranian Zoroastrians were forced into inland enclaves such as Yazd and Kerman. This minute geographic difference escalated into a much larger economic disparity in a world economy heavily reliant on trade — and it is unlikely that even had Iranian Zoroastrians been located near a port, the Arab Caliphates would have allowed them to trade independently and retain the profits. The Parsees' proximity to the sea, combined with the societal freedom to build their own economic systems and retain profits without heavy taxation, gave them an early economic base that later enabled trade with, and access to schooling and jobs from, the British upon their arrival. The second piece of comparative analysis concerns setting: Iranian Zoroastrians worked blue-collar jobs, surrounded only by other Iranian Zoroastrians in similarly unfavourable economic conditions, while the Parsees surrounded themselves with the British, trading with and working alongside British managers in institutions like the East India Company — a setting that was a key factor in future opportunity. Based on geography and surrounding groups, the Parsees acquired a comparative head start in education and English literacy that advanced into higher-paying jobs, creating the economic divide between the two communities — and this economic factor is itself one of the reasons the two groups rarely interact today, as one side has restricted geographical mobility due to lower disposable income.

### Cultural and Demographic Differences

The cultural differences between the Parsees and Iranian Zoroastrians have influenced how both groups have practiced and preached their religion from the seventh century AD to the modern day. In synthesis, Iranian Zoroastrians preserved traditions to a greater extent due to forced insularity, while the Parsees, without deviating extensively, incorporated aspects of Indian culture into their religious practice and let go of some traditionally Persian aspects.

Despite retaining religious freedom in principle, Iranian Zoroastrians faced many barriers to open practice, given the broader culture of oppression against non-Muslims in Iran, limiting their religious practice to private spaces. The Yasna ceremony, a daily staple of Zoroastrian culture, migrated into individual households, allowing small differences to emerge between how each household performed it — though the sheer perseverance of continuing to practice behind closed doors is itself evidence of the strength of Iranian Zoroastrian cultural and religious preservation despite oppression.

Concurrently in India, public religious practice was the societal norm. In Gujarat during the 7th–10th centuries, Chaulukya rulers openly advocated for and tolerated religious freedom. The Yasna was performed in newly built fire temples in open community spaces, the most popular being the Sanjan Atash Behram — a scale of communal worship previously unheard of in Persia, where most Atash Behrams were built to serve a single town or village. It was Hindu influence, and the large open religious gatherings of the surrounding Hindu population in Gujarat, that shaped this new standard of large-scale religious gathering among the Parsees, one that persists to this day, aided further by the economic stability from trade that allowed for larger, more numerous temples than could be built in Yazd or Kerman. These were among the first cultural adaptations that shaped a distinctly Parsee identity — not fully Indian, and not fully Persian Zoroastrian, but a fusion of the two.

These larger, more open daily rituals also set a new standard for festivals. The largest celebration, then and now, is Nowruz (Persian New Year), the most important festival in the Persian calendar, occurring at least once and sometimes twice a year and bringing together whole communities — making it a valuable point of comparison between the two groups. The Iranian Zoroastrians' Nowruz celebration overlaps with the Parsees' Jamshedi Navroz, both falling at the spring equinox by the Fasli calendar; the Parsees also celebrate a second Navroz, Shahanshahi Navroz, in August, based on the older Shahanshahi calendar that was less commonly used in Persia but was adopted by the Parsees during their migration to India — suggesting the Iranian Zoroastrians hold closer ties to their Persian history and are more reluctant to adapt than the Parsees, who added a second calendar altogether. The manner of practice also differs: in Iran, Zoroastrians treat Nowruz as a chance to reconnect with God, spending the day in prayer in small groups at local Atash Behrams, while in India there is a lesser focus on prayer and a stronger focus on welcoming the new year through singing, dancing, and large communal dinners — a longer-term impact of Hindu-influenced traditions of large religious gatherings.

Demographically, the Iranian Zoroastrians have had comparatively smaller but more stable numbers than the Parsees in India. The 2013 Iranian census counted 25,000 Zoroastrians in Iran, while the Federation of Zoroastrian Associations of North America (FEZANA) estimated closer to 40,000 — an underreporting gap of roughly 38%. Since neither body has an obvious incentive to misrepresent the figures (if anything, the Iranian government might have reason to overreport, given its interest in projecting diversity on the world stage), it is likely the individuals themselves — reluctant to be flagged as non-Muslim and subject to higher taxation — are the less objective party, choosing not to distinguish themselves from wider Iranian society when officially counted, but more willing to self-identify to FEZANA, where there is no fear of government retaliation. This further supports the idea of cultural and societal oppression in Iran, though it also suggests a semi-positive point: while underreported, the numbers are staying broadly stable over time, suggesting societal treatment of the minority is not visibly worsening.

Analogously, in India, numbers are larger overall but declining: the 2001 Indian census reported 76,000 Zoroastrians, while the 2011 census reported only 57,000 — a 17% decline in a decade. Underreporting is unlikely here, given the religious freedoms and economic benefits documented above; instead, this decline stems from endogamy. Cultural assimilation among Parsees in India has led to increasingly common inter-faith marriage, and because the Bombay Parsee Panchayat's rules only recognise a child as Parsee if the father is Zoroastrian, such children are restricted from joining the community from birth. The openness of Indian society has thus given the Parsees mobility, while simultaneously opening them to cultural assimilation that may be tipping into cultural erosion.

In summary, while the isolation of Iran restricted the Iranian Zoroastrians from economic prosperity and social integration, it allowed them to preserve their Zoroastrian culture to a greater degree than the Parsees of India were able to. In terms of population, neither country has seen growth, with decline most visible in India — though it remains crucial to weigh the validity of sources here, since underreporting in Iran leaves open the possibility of actual growth there. Even so, a growing population that lacks the freedom to openly practice and preach its religion raises real questions about the religion's long-term prospects.

## Conclusion

To conclude, let's first synthesize the findings of this paper. Section one established the theology of the Zoroastrian religion and linked its susceptibility to division back to an overly strong linkage between the geographical location of its followers and the religion itself. Section two built on those findings to explore raw data and information on the lives of citizens in both countries, extrapolating the implicit and explicit divides between the two communities from societal, economic, and cultural lenses. Together, these sections show that in the wake of the Arab conquest of 651 CE, there remain prevalent economic, societal, and cultural divides between the Zoroastrian populations residing in India and in Iran.

The broader implication of this study speaks to the religious understanding of post-conquest religions generally, and can help religions navigate the challenges that present themselves when portions of their population migrate to a new location. It also has current implications for the Zoroastrian community specifically, providing a baseline understanding of the challenges it faces that could inform long-term efforts to improve the religion's longevity and demographic stability.

As with all research, this paper faces certain limitations. Possible bias in sources such as the Qissa-i-Sanjan, consistently referred to throughout the paper, makes it difficult to have a fully objective view of the historical settings and situations the Parsees found themselves in. The possible fear of identifying as Zoroastrian in Iran is another limitation on the sources used for demographic analysis. While these are the best sources available at the time of publication, they cannot be considered fully objective or complete — this paper should therefore not be read as absolute fact, but as the best possible overview of the Zoroastrian divergence between these two communities.
