This research report investigates to what extent the trade policies of the British East India Company (BEIC) between 1608 and 1874 have hindered India's local industrial progress. The BEIC was established by Queen Elizabeth I as the first joint-stock company, and as one of the largest corporations of its time, the East India Company dominated over 50% of global trade during the mid-1700s and early 1800s between Europe, South Asia, and the Far East, exploiting modern trade routes and redirecting returns to Britain, flourishing British industrialisation. In the process, it played a central role in the colonization of modern-day India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Burma.

Many Indian historians argue that the BEIC's methods to prevent Indian industrialisation were focused on increased taxation. However, this paper argues that the BEIC's demand for changing manufacturing from finished goods to raw materials was a key factor as well — through utilizing local markets to advocate British finished goods whilst shifting Indian manufacturing solely to raw material production. Through reviewing the existing literature and conducting deep research regarding industrial growth, and by using significant primary data collections and secondary quantitative data regarding trade data, taxation influx, cotton prices, and qualitative research regarding handicraft extinction and job displacement, this paper highlights how dominant powers seize and utilise prominent policies to eradicate profits, steal resources, and maximise self-demand and exposure to trade.

This report examines the impact of the British East India Company's colonial rule on industrialization in India between 1608 and 1874. It explores key historical events that contributed to the stagnation of Indian industries under British rule, highlighting how dominant colonial powers leveraged economic policies to suppress local enterprise and industrialization. The analysis underscores the systematic exploitation of resources, the imposition of excessive taxation, the decline of indigenous handicrafts, and the displacement of labor — strategies designed to maximize British economic interests while stifling India's industrial growth.

## Section 1: Taxation Duties on Key Industries

Taxation and tariff implementations played a significant role in restricting industrial growth. During British colonial rule, the British East India Company promulgated high tariffs on Indian finished goods. This crippled local industrial manufacturing, inclusive of cotton textiles, and expanded to other artisanal industries. Additionally, export profits were redirected to Britain, further strengthening its economy at the expense of India's industrial progress. The highly profitable textile industry within India's economy faced steep taxation increases of 70–80% on its goods, specifically local cotton production and handicrafts, as a result of their unique qualities compelling European demand through their authentic designs and rich quality. Its distribution within local trade markets further suppressed local industrialization.

Heavy financial burdens severely restricted textile manufacturing exports while maximizing profits for the British East India Company and British industrial growth. Indian textiles, specifically exquisite cotton fabrics like muslin and calico, sparked highly unanticipated demand in Europe. Regional demand was inspired by the idiosyncratic local Indian artisanal designs exported from the 1680s. Raw cotton production from India only competed at approximately 30% to 55% of British raw cotton prices between 1710 and 1823. British weavers and textile manufacturers thus struggled in production, in competition against the rich-heavy meritoriousness and cost-effective consumption of Indian cotton textiles and artisanal industries.

In the early 1700s, India contributed 27% of global textile exports, positioning its textile industry as a vision for local industrialization, but this dropped to 2% during the post-rule of the BEIC. The BEIC's parliament members' expansion enforced political decision-making and increased unrequited exports back to the British economy. Systematically eradicating local industrialization, they eroded India's global market share, crippling its once-dominant manufacturing sector. Unrequited exports transported to Britain subsequently increased industrial debt within cross-industries, specifically with handicrafts and cotton. Subsequent law enforcement limited firm expandability and directed lucrative efficacy to European markets, specifically Great Britain, further emphasizing selfish British redirection of profit and utilizing the trade monopoly to exploit India's trading exports, deducting crucial visionaries of growth for key industries. India started serving as a manufacturing country under the British Raj, manufacturing products inclusive of cotton, silk, indigo, tea, and saltpeter for gunpowder creation for other nations, and expanding the business's trade systems from local spices to weaponry. These cumulatively increased profit, tax revenue, and expenditure — by 1744, the British had made 70 grams of gold from tax income depleted from Indian profitability.

India's dependency on local European demand was taken advantage of by the BEIC's enforced law entitlements. Laws like the Calico Act (1700 & 1721) banned all imports of finished cotton textiles to Britain, damaging industrial manufacturing, and indicating that local raw cotton be exported to Britain and sold from then forward. By 1840, Indian cotton textiles exported to Britain faced a 10% tax, silk 20%, and wool 30%, while British products entering India were taxed at only 3.5%. Economist Bishnupriya Gupta suggests, "The changes in the international textile market have been a central issue in explaining the rise of Britain as the industrial power." The BEIC's unrequited exports subsequently increased industrial stagnancy, hindering globally acknowledged Indian firms from contributing to India's long-term economic and industrial stability. Additionally, Britain successfully increased the share it had taken from cotton exports of the Indian economy: in 1810 the British occupied 0.1% of shares for cotton textiles sold, yet by 1890, in the post-colonial period, the British accounted for 51.8% of shares of the average annual Indian consumption of local cotton textiles.

Analyzing the numerical increases in taxation and tariff rates during the BEIC's rule from 1604 to 1874 shows the devastating role they played in crippling India's industrial growth. High tariffs on Indian textiles, specifically cotton, combined with unrequited exporting to Britain, led to the decline of India's once-thriving manufacturing sector, stifling local industry and displacing skilled workers into unstable agricultural labor, deepening rural poverty. British colonial control over the country's industrialization was a key advantage for British gain — these policies exacerbated and dismantled industrialization, economic growth, and wealth within India's landscape.

## Section 2: Labour Market Displacement and Handicraft Extinction

The British East India Company's taxation policies not only hindered industrial growth but also oppressed individual economic freedom, forcing workers into exploitative labor conditions. As India's industrial strain intensified, over 68% of the workforce suffered from labor displacement and were transferred into the unpredictable agricultural sector during the post-rule period of the BEIC around 1900. Amidst the BEIC's heavy tax duties on Indian products, combined with cheap British imports, traditional industry modernization of exquisites like textiles and handicrafts significantly declined. The East India Company's colonial rule not only hindered industrial growth but also expanded its oppressive policies to other sectors, particularly unique artisanal businesses.

The British East India Company's land reform policies engaged subsequent colonial monopolies to concentrate BEIC profitability by hindering industrial growth. Among three specific systems, the Permanent Settlement, initiated by Lord Cornwallis, had the most significant impact on industrial growth during the British East India Company rule. This Settlement used zamindars — landowning intermediaries, typically members of the landed aristocracy or wealthy merchants — who were granted the right to collect revenue from peasants. These zamindars exploited peasants by demanding higher rents, pushing them into debt and serfdom. This reduced the purchasing power of a large segment of the population, limiting domestic demand for industrial goods. Zamindars focused solely on extracting rent and made no efforts to improve land or agricultural practices, and this stagnation in agricultural productivity hindered the development of agro-based industries.

These weren't the only operating systems. The Mahalwari system, introduced in the northwestern provinces, and the Ryotwari system, initiated by Sir Thomas Munro, were other land revenue systems established to create a more equitable and efficient land revenue collection process. These two systems generally allowed for more flexibility and direct engagement with cultivators, potentially fostering a more conducive environment for economic diversification and growth — including increasing government revenue and the cultivation of barren land to encourage agricultural production. It is therefore notable that the Permanent Settlement, among the systems initiated during colonial rule, was the most significant in hindering industrialization.

The agricultural system had already been stressed by British-imposed land taxes and policies. Certain systematic policies forced farmers into growing crops for British export rather than prioritising local consumption. Consequently, food shortages and high prices became commonplace, leading to widespread famine and malnutrition in certain regions. While British companies profited from the exportation of raw materials, including cotton, jute, and spices, India was left to cope with the economic fallout, as agricultural production could not meet the basic needs of the population. The forced transition from a diverse economy — once rich in craftsmanship, trade, and industry — into a supplier of raw materials left a deep scar on India's economic development. Traditional crafts and industries were not just economically suppressed — they were culturally stunted as well.

Once-thriving artisan industries, which had been crucial to India's social and cultural fabric, faced extinction, replaced by mass-produced foreign goods that lacked the artistry and uniqueness of traditional Indian craftsmanship. With its political dominance over India's trade and industries, the BEIC systematically drained local profits, suppressed entrepreneurship, and stifled economic development, leaving once-thriving sectors in a state of decline. British colonial policies played a pivotal role in deepening rural poverty by systematically dismantling India's industrial base. The wealth produced by cotton textiles was heavily redirected towards food production exports to supply companies in Britain, while agricultural cultivation could not provide individuals with their necessities, financially destabilizing workers and long-term economic growth. The Indian economy grew about 1% per year from 1880 to 1920, and the population also grew by 1% — the result was, on average, no long-term change in income levels. Agriculture was still dominant, with most peasants at the subsistence level. Extensive irrigation systems were built, providing an impetus for growing cash crops for export and raw materials for Indian industry, especially jute, cotton, sugarcane, coffee, and tea.

Therefore, the East India Company's economic policies severely undermined India's industrial potential and stunted its agricultural system, leaving the country trapped in a cycle of exploitation and poverty. By prioritizing British profits over Indian well-being, the BEIC not only crushed local industries and crafts but also decimated the livelihoods of millions. The Company's forceful implementations of law enforcement sparked job displacement and hindered valued industries like handicrafts and textiles.

## Section 3: From Finished Goods to Raw Material Production

During the BEIC's rule, raw material production in India was among the most affordable and authentic. This successful production was conserved in India's production of finished goods, sparking market appreciation for the Indian economy. Britain seized this opportunity to expand British trade by invading the Indian market, gaining assets from unrequited exports following the trade monopoly's devastating tax burdens on the economy.

By the end of 1875, nearing the BEIC's retreat, the share of Indian market goods made by the British accounted for 55% of goods sold, prioritizing British production and stagnating Indian profitability and industrialization across cross-industries within the country's landscape. Britain's constant enhancement and understanding of the value of finished goods in India enabled the BEIC to scale up raw-material production instead, substituting factors like British cotton after transporting exports without consent back to the British economy. The BEIC deliberately changed industrial production to raw material manufacturing, such as gold and raw cotton, for finished goods to be produced elsewhere, particularly in Europe. This was not only a distinct way to occupy Indian trade, but it also maximized profit for the British, as affordable materials from India reduced fixed and variable costs for firm production and revenue.

The British East India Company's strategy wasn't just about controlling land — it was about controlling economic power. By shifting India from an industrial hub to a resource colony, Britain ensured its economic dominance while keeping India underdeveloped. This economic structure persisted even after the BEIC dissolved, laying the foundation for India's struggle with poverty and industrial lag even after independence in 1947. Its cycle revolved around three key steps: extracting raw materials from India (such as cotton and gold); shipping these materials to Britain for manufacturing; and selling the finished products back to India at a higher price.

Additionally, the Indian population's reluctant dependency on these thriving industries soon rendered them redundant as the trade monopoly seized industry manufacturing, resorting to globally demanding products as its own firms. With little incentive or resources to develop its industries, India became economically and financially reliant on Britain, while British industries flourished, benefitting from the cheap raw materials produced in India. This system of economic exploitation was designed to maximize British profits by lowering production costs through access to inexpensive Indian materials while keeping India in a perpetual state of underdevelopment. As raw materials were unrequitedly exported from Indian producers with little compensation for the labor-intensive work, the wealth generated from these resources was siphoned off to benefit Britain. Furthermore, the country's industrial purpose changed from flourishing textiles and handcrafted artisanal goods to predominantly raw material exportation, minimising export profitability and correlating with Britain's long-term development advantage. The monopoly's occupation of local firms under the British Crown favoured exploitative policies and political decision-making that tangled the industrialisation process within a cycle revolving around raw material manufacturing, particularly in favour of the British — leaving the country's unique artisanal and textile firms redundant.

## Conclusion

The colonial monopoly's seizure over Indian industrialisation was an event that will forever be known through India's industrial progression throughout the 21st century and beyond. This paper looked into how raw material production was the unnoticed reason for industrial hindrance, contributing more than the more-researched possibilities of this effect, inclusive of handicraft extinction, job displacement, and taxation increase — which may remain debatable, as individuals may opt to see this hindrance through a different lens. Politicians, specifically in Britain, may see otherwise regarding their colonial establishment of the British East India Company between 1608 and 1874. This report examines how dominant powers exploit policies to extract profits, resources, and trade advantages, hindering India's industrial progress. It critiques unfair political supremacy that enforces economic dependency and societal harm.
