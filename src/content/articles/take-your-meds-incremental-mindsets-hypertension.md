Non-adherence within hypertensive patient populations is a prevalent but complex issue that stems from both constitutional failures and personal decisions. There are three most prominent pillars that contribute to this phenomenon: communicational, socioeconomic and motivational factors. This paper explores the possible application of a growth mindset in resolving motivational issues by increasing the perceived value of treatment and fostering perseverance when faced with medical roadblocks. However, other contributors to non-adherence, such as socioeconomic and communicational barriers, limit the effect of growth mindset application.

## Introduction

Hypertension is a highly prevalent medical problem that occurs when blood pressure in the vessels is consistently elevated. WHO estimates that there were around 1.4 billion people living with hypertension in 2024.1 Although frequently asymptomatic for most individuals, untreated hypertension significantly increases the risk of stroke, heart attacks, chronic kidney disease, and dementia. Treatment of hypertension relies on lifelong medication and potential lifestyle changes, requiring dedication and consistency in treatment plans and schedules. However, a key issue emerges: patient non-adherence to therapy regimens.2

Non-adherence refers to an observed phenomenon in which patients do not follow prescribed treatment recommendations from their healthcare provider. Individuals who do not follow medication plans, either by not initiating medication or by discontinuing early, often see the poorest clinical outcomes and may result in severe side effects.3 Non-adherence to medicine is a global issue and 43%–65.5% of noncompliant patients are hypertensive patients who do not adhere strictly to their prescribed medications as recommended,4 highlighting a widespread issue not only as a clinical failure resulting in preventable cardiovascular events but also as a massive economic burden on healthcare infrastructure.5 In order to resolve this issue, researchers have grouped barriers to maintaining medication regimens into three key sectors: socioeconomic, communicational, and motivational. It is important to acknowledge the root cause of noncompliance in order to effectively examine how noncompliance can be reduced.

While studies have focused extensively on key factors contributing to, and methods to identify, non-adherence, little research has been conducted to examine the effectiveness of mindset changes on improving adherence. Despite its usual application in child psychology and education, current literature suggests that exhibiting a growth mindset can greatly improve adherence to therapy in a medical setting. According to Implicit Theories of Intelligence, mindsets (referred to as implicit theories) are responsible for both motivational and cognitive frameworks that influence the individuals' views and responses to learning engagement and achievement.6,7 They define an individual with a growth mindset (referred to as an incremental theorist) as focusing on achieving mastery in subject areas and using performance outcomes as feedback opportunities to self-reflect. In contrast, fixed-mindset individuals (referred to as entity theorists) believe intelligence to be a static, immutable quality, treating performance opportunities as a definitive statement of their ability. Entity theorists give up prematurely and lose motivation if results do not align with expectations, rather than practising reflection and improving upon faults.8,9

Another key factor that predicts student learning and achievement is the possession of intrinsic motivation, defined as a force that encourages behaviour and self-determination due to inherent enjoyment.10 Its counterpart, extrinsic motivation, is the driving force to achieve something for the reward offered for completing the task.11 Of the two, intrinsic motivation has been deemed to be responsible for lifelong learning by the general academia, as it encourages perseverance despite external setbacks.10

The application of incremental theories and encouragement of intrinsic motivation improves patient adherence in a healthcare setting by resolving the motivation barrier. As identified earlier, patient motivation is a significant contributor to non-adherence, as they might underestimate the importance of completing treatment plans. Hypertension is also classified as an oligosymptomatic disorder, which is a disorder that generally shows mild symptoms; therefore, long-lasting therapy regimens that may become repetitive or seem unrewarding over time. This view likely stems from the lack of external validation associated with curative results such as a major decrease in symptoms and discomfort; hence, patients become dissuaded. Therefore, this paper seeks to investigate the extent to which adopting an incremental mindset can improve therapy adherence in patients with severe hypertension by increasing the perceived value of prescribed medical therapy. However, it is also important to acknowledge that patient non-adherence stems from many holistic factors that may become barriers to following therapy regimens rather than a conscious choice to stop taking medication.12

Throughout this paper, the root causes of non-adherence are going to be thoroughly examined in the context of hypertensive patients in order to holistically understand how it could be effectively reduced in patient populations. Growth mindsets and intrinsic motivation are then going to be introduced and reviewed in their original context of education, leading to evaluations on the potential application of these interventions in decreasing noncompliance in the context of medicine. Factors that limit the effectiveness of growth mindset application will also be thoroughly identified and explained.

## Main Causes of Non-Adherence

Understanding the causes of non-adherence is fundamental to analysing the effectiveness of growth mindset therapy on improving medication adherence. The oligosymptomatic nature of this disease requires intrinsic dedication in both lifestyle and medication compliance for long periods of time, although treatment may appear unsatisfactory.13

A current method to explain causes of patient non-adherence can be attributed to the introduction of the Health Belief Model (HBM) by social psychologists in the 1950s. Through multi-faceted assessments in areas of perceptions such as predisposition, significance, gravity, benefits and obstacles to a health behaviour, the HBM can accurately examine and predict individual behaviours relating to prevention and control of disease and symptoms. Rosenstock et al. synthesised that treatment compliance is heavily reliant on perceived susceptibility, perceived significance, perceived obstacles, perceived benefits and cues to action (Figure 1.). At its core, the three main pillars of non-adherence can be summarised as motivation, belief of vulnerability, and trust in recommendations.

While it was originally used to assess patient motives behind an individual's participation in health programmes, a study by Joho (2021) examined how key factors of HBM can influence treatment compliance within hypertensive patients in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. In her study, a cross-dimensional approach was used to analyse data from 3 regional hospitals in Dar es Salaam. Random sampling was used to select hypertensive patients which met the inclusion criteria, which required patients to be at least 18 years and above with an existing diagnosis of hypertension for at least one month (therefore have been taking anti-hypertensive treatment for at least one month prior to the beginning of the study), regardless of whether they had a coexisting medical condition. All participants have consented to participate in the study.5

Data were collected in areas of perceived significance, benefit, barriers, lifestyle modifications and adherence to therapy regimens in the form of survey questions, in which participants responded with decreasing frequency. For example, a question on treatment compliance, asking "How often do you forget to take your medicine?", is scored from 1-4 on a 4-point Likert scale: "1" every day, "2" frequently, "3" rarely or "4" never. However, this meant that the highest score may not be entirely indicative of non-compliance. This is illustrated by the question "How often do you engage in physical exercise?" for which a higher score of "4" indicates better compliance as opposed to "1". In these cases, scores were reversed for more accurate scoring.

The results from the study demonstrate a positive relationship between perceived severity, perceived benefit and treatment compliance. Participants with high perceived severity (57.4% compliant) and benefit (66.7% compliant) were more likely to follow treatment regimens than participants without (53.7% and 44.9% compliant, respectively). As expected, a negative relationship was shown between perceived barriers and treatment compliance. A lower perceived barrier predicted higher treatment compliance, at 77%, compared to those with a high perceived barrier, who had only 29.5% compliance. The study also revealed that compliance can be heavily influenced by socio-demographical factors such as sex. Female participants (63%) reported a higher percentage of compliance compared to male participants (46%). The other socio-demographical factors such as marital status, age, education, and occupation were also explored but did not appear statistically significant.

These findings lead to the conclusion that perceived susceptibility, benefit, and barriers are all key contributing factors to patient non-adherence in treatment regimens. Although some socio-demographical factors such as martial status, age, education and employment were not statistically significant within this experiment, they may be important determinants of compliance for individuals living outside of Dar es Salaam, Tanzania.

A study14 on cost-related medication noncompliance (CRN) using data from the National Health Interview Survey found that medication unaffordability was the strongest predictor of CRN, showing consistent findings along with another study by McHorney15 in 2008 and by McHorney et al.16 in 2010. These results suggest that unaffordability of healthcare may not be a prevalent issue in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania; hence, differences in employment statuses were not statistically significant. However, as demonstrated by the conclusion from the above studies, socioeconomic status is an important determinant of adherence for the rest of the world.

Another model used to demonstrate and explain factors of non-adherence is the Venn diagram model (Figure 2.) constructed by Baroletti17 et al. in 2010. This model highlights the three pillars of treatment noncompliance as socioeconomic, motivational and communicational barriers.

*Figure 2. Demonstrating the factors contributing to non-adherence and overlaps.17*

As illustrated by Figure 2, socioeconomic barriers are determined by the financial background of individuals, specifically an individual's inability to afford medication. Consistent with previous studies on CRN,14 patients who struggle with high costs of chronic treatment may involuntarily choose to drop medication plans, despite having full awareness of the consequences if they choose to do so.16 Chronic conditions such as hypertension further amplify this issue and in addition to inadequate healthcare coverage, long-term treatment medication costs can seem like a financially insurmountable obstacle.

Communication barriers can be attributed to the difficulty in understanding treatment plans, often resulting in mistrust, misunderstanding, or misconception. The many sub-factors that contribute to communication issues between provider and patient can be organised into two main groups: Difficulty understanding instructions or low levels of trust with the provider. A patient may face obstacles with understanding instructions if they are low in functional fluency of the language, are of elder age or face mental illness or substance abuse.12 Mistrust may also occur if patients face discriminatory behaviours enacted by providers, with 53% of patients reporting non-adherence to physician recommendations.18

Consistent with the conclusion drawn from the HBM study,5 motivation to follow treatment regimens is a crucial factor in determining behaviours of non-adherence. As identified earlier, the 4 main determinants are perceived susceptibility, perceived significance, perceived obstacles and perceived benefits of medication. In this model, perceived susceptibility and significance can be classified as having a poor understanding of the disease, while fear of adverse effects could count as an obstacle to compliance.

It is equally important to acknowledge the overlaps between the three main pillars in order to holistically examine the causes of non-adherence. As illustrated by Figure 2, a high need for monitoring may cause both socioeconomic and communicational pressure on the patient. Difficulty in understanding treatment plans could cause unintentional non-adherence due to a lack of perceived need for self-monitoring and language barriers cause difficulty rescheduling or understanding check-up timings.19

Medical appointments could also become financially taxing on individuals if there is inadequate healthcare coverage. Limited time spent with a patient can contribute to both communicational and motivational barriers, as there may not be enough time to fully explain the importance of therapy regimens or how to take them. This can cause a lack of perceived significance of medication and poor understanding of disease progression. The issue is amplified when medication regimens are complex and difficult to follow, causing unintentional non-adherence if patients do not understand how to take medication appropriately. Confusion caused by unclear instructions may contribute to a poor understanding of the illness. Polypharmacy, defined as regularly using five or more medications in the same time period,20 is an issue that may intensify both socioeconomic and motivation barriers within patient communities. This is due to a decrease in the perceived need for taking extra medication and the accumulation of such drugs may be financially taxing overtime, especially if they are all used to manage chronic conditions.

While models thoroughly explain all holistic factors that contribute to medication noncompliance for hypertensive patient communities, all reasons can be summarised into three main pillars: motivational, socioeconomic, and communicational barriers. The majority of non-adherence stems from a lack of perceived need and benefit for taking medication; therefore, it is crucial to explore affordable methods to mitigate these motivational obstacles. A potential procedure could be the adoption of an incremental mindset by patients to enhance their intrinsic motivation and perceived value of taking medication.

## Incremental Theory and Intrinsic Motivation

Understanding the theory behind incremental theories and intrinsic motivation is crucial to analysing its potential application in the healthcare field as a possible method to mitigate motivational issues. Incremental theory, commonly known as growth mindset theory, has been a common focus of research regarding topics in child psychology and learning.

Students who hold incremental mindsets do not view mistakes as definitive judgements of their ability,6,7 treating learning as an opportunity for growth rather than a testament to one's intelligence. This approach increases the perceived value of the learning process instead of the result, shifting the focus from performance to mastery. Many positive correlations have been found between adopting an incremental approach and higher academic achievement,21,22 likely stemming from the belief that poor performance does not define their ability and instead offers valuable feedback for improvement. Findings from a study by Huang et al.23 in 2022 suggest that students who are incremental theorists believe in the importance of effort and persistence in improving abilities, which ultimately enhances their sense of self-determination and intrinsic motivation to learn. This belief enables students to retain high academic performance even with the removal of extrinsic rewards such as academic awards, developing essential lifelong learning habits.10 Students who hold incremental mindsets may also be less likely to succumb to academic obstacles, instead looking for solutions to resolve barriers due to an intrinsic drive to learn.

Intrinsic motivation, therefore, is often positively associated with growth mindsets. It is defined as the inherent satisfaction derived from performing an activity without external incentives,10 completing tasks due to their own enjoyment and genuine interest.24 It has become an important determinant in engagement in tasks that require persistence and high levels of efforts to achieve,24 suggesting that intrinsic motivation often plays a key role in resilience when confronting barriers and obstacles faced throughout learning.

While incremental theory and intrinsic motivation are fundamentally different concepts, they are both factors that increase the inherent enjoyment of activities and the motivation to complete them. Although incremental theory and intrinsic motivation are incredibly beneficial tools for student performance, they are also important factors which contribute to lifelong learning and increase persistence when faced with challenges and setbacks.

## Application of Incremental Theory and Intrinsic Motivation in Improving Non-Adherence

While incremental theory and intrinsic motivation are historically rooted in educational settings, their core principles may help patients navigate around motivational barriers that arise from managing a chronic physiological condition like hypertension. As identified previously, motivational non-adherence for hypertensive patients stems from the lack of perceived value or need for completing their medication.17 This could be attributed to the oligosymptomatic nature of the disease, requiring long-lasting therapy that does not show immediate or satisfactory results, diminishing the perceived urgency of completing therapy schemes.13 It is also noted by Joho5 that an individual's perceived barrier to completing a medication course can be an important determinant in compliance. Hence, it is crucial to examine how incremental theory and intrinsic motivation could help alleviate these challenges.

As previously mentioned, intrinsic motivation plays a key role in regulating behaviours and satisfaction derived from completing a task. Intrinsically motivated individuals are driven to perform activities due to inherent enjoyment and high perceived value; therefore, external rewards are often not needed. In a medical setting, increasing the percieved value of taking medication could significantly improve patient adherence despite low-to-no external validation (i.e., symptom relief).

A study by Boaler25 demonstrated individuals with a fixed mindset are less likely to persevere through challenges or respond critically to feedback due to the belief that capability and ability cannot be changed. In a healthcare setting, patients with fixed mindsets could be more likely to shy away from challenges or inconveniences that arise from adapting to the treatment regimen, for example, when management requires making lifestyle changes. Low perceived urgency and unsatisfactory results make compliance seem like an unnecessary burden, rather than recognising benefits in the long term.

However, by adopting a growth mindset, these challenges could potentially be mitigated. This is because individuals who possess implicit theories view challenges as an important step of their learning journey, as they believe that feedback received from evaluation opportunities can lead to improvement. This makes them more likely to confront and persist with solving obstacles compared to fixed mindset individuals, who believe that skill and talent are stagnant and cannot be altered with persistence and effort.26 For a hypertensive patient, this may translate to viewing obstacles in their medication journey as a crucial opportunity for feedback response rather than an insurmountable roadblock. Alterations in lifestyle may be viewed as an opportunity to improve living conditions rather than a troublesome disruption in daily routines. Patients may also feel less intimidated by fear of potential challenges and be more willing to find alternative solutions instead of stopping treatment all together.

In essence, the lack of motivation to adhere to an asymptomatic treatment plan is fundamentally caused by low levels of perceived need to comply and shying away from potential obstacles. This leads to the conclusion that adoption of an incremental approach could intrinsically incentivise patients to complete therapy schemes despite differences between perceived benefit and actual benefit.

## Limitations of Applications of Incremental Theories

While growth mindset theory may be a possible solution to reducing motivational non-adherence, it is equally important to examine the potential limitations when it comes to reducing other barriers. As examined in Part 1, non-adherence is a multidimensional issue that arises from complex difficulties in three major areas: motivation, socioeconomic status and communication. Addressing these issues may involve constitutional changes in healthcare systems that require a large amount of effort and may take a long time.

For example, the majority of socioeconomic non-adherence stems from the inability to afford medication for long therapeutic time frames, often amplified when polypharmacy is involved. Addressing this issue may require reforming health policies, such as price regulation strategies27 or providing healthcare coverage. Unemployment and poverty are also crucial determinants of non-adherence, as they are directly correlated with one's ability to afford medication for the duration of treatment. Reducing these factors may require systematic changes within government policies, such as providing benefits and pensions for those in need.

The majority of communicational non-adherence is caused by the inability to understand treatment plans, resulting in mistrust, misconception, and misunderstanding between provider and patient. This issue usually arises when there are language barriers that prevent effective communication, which requires systematic changes in the healthcare system such as employing more native-speaker doctors in areas that do not have English as an official language. Discriminatory behaviours enacted by providers can also greatly impact trust between patient-doctor relationships. This can be reduced by integrating anti-discriminatory classes into training programmes or fostering accountability for discriminatory practices.28

Non-adherence is a complex issue that requires both individual and systematic changes, which may take long periods of time to implement and take effect. Although growth mindsets can be effectively used to reduce motivational challenges, it is also important to recognise that the observed phenomenon of non-adherence can reveal implicit flaws within healthcare systems.

## Conclusion

This literature demonstrates that the inherent link between intrinsic motivation and incremental theory holds significant application potential in medicine-related fields to reduce patient non-adherence, specifically by increasing both the internal drive to complete ongoing therapy plans without a direct external reward and the willingness to persevere despite obstacles encountered throughout treatment. However, external factors such as inability to afford long-term medication, lack of trust between provider and patient, and communication difficulties are also key contributors to non-adherence. These usually require constitutional changes within healthcare systems, such as making medication more affordable, resolving language barriers, and anti-discrimination training. For patient populations with sufficient functional literacy and the financial capability to afford sufficient medication, adopting an incremental mindset can be an effective strategy to manage motivation and routine-building. In comparison to other alternatives, fostering a growth mindset within patients can be more cost-effective, more affordable, and offer benefits outside of healthcare settings.
