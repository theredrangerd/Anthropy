This paper examines the role played by the Indian Army in the integration of the Princely States into the Indian Union between 1947 and 1950. While India is a republic with minimal military influence in civilian affairs, this period saw the Army take on constabulary functions. This paper is framed through Janowitz's theory of the military's transition to a constabulary force, and highlights the role of the Army in the stabilisation of the subcontinent. These findings challenge the notion that the Army was inconsequential in the formation of the Indian Union, and have considerable implications for the modern-day Indian military, as it demonstrates how the Indian military's actions between 1947–1950 shaped India's actions in later conflicts with both China and Pakistan, and how they helped to ensure that the military was that of a democratic nation — one that did not interfere in civil affairs.

## Introduction

India's government is not a junta, stratocracy, or military dictatorship. Instead, it is a republic. As such, India's military has little to no influence over civilian affairs, with the military's role being strictly limited to national defense and maintaining peace and security within the country. This is unlike other nations where the military has high influence over civilian affairs. From the early days of Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru, the Indian political system has deliberately maintained strong civilian leadership at the highest levels, ensuring all decisions taken on national security are taken by elected officials. This democratic structure contrasts with that of other nations, in which militaries play a substantial role in internal politics and policy decisions. Though this has been the case for much of India's history after independence, the Indian Army played a critical role in India's early days and in her formation, through events that transpired during the period following independence. The Indian Army was instrumental in the accession of the princely states — where local rulers of more than five hundred states were given the option to accede to either India or Pakistan.

This paper examines the role of the Indian Army in the integration of the Princely States into India, and how its civil and military operations contributed to India's formation as a state during 1947–1950, through close analysis of primary and secondary sources including journals, museum websites, and scholarly articles, while contextualising these developments in India's civil-military relations through Janowitz's framework of a military evolving from a combat force to a constabulary force.

While some argue that the Indian Army did not have a major role in India's formation as a state, due to the civilian government's primacy over the military, the Indian Army played a pivotal role in the integration of the Princely States through key operations and attempts to maintain control after a bloody partition. While the former idea has some merit, as armies of other countries with a military dictatorship have occupied a greater role in the politics of their respective nations, the disorganisation of the Indian civil government, especially in once-disputed territories such as Junagadh and Kashmir, led to the Indian Army taking on the role of a constabulary force.

## Evolution of the Indian Military from the British-Indian Military

In order to understand the role of the Indian Army during and after partition, one must first understand the British-Indian Army and its evolution into independent India's Army after independence in 1947. In his seminal work *The Professional Soldier*, Morris Janowitz (1971) proposed the theory that in all democratic nations, the military would evolve from being a force solely focused on traditional warfare to a constabulary force, with tasks such as internal security and peacekeeping. India's military evolution during this period was similar to the evolution proposed by Janowitz, wherein a military that was originally focused on external conquest gradually assumed roles similar to that of a constabulary force. Although this theory was proposed in a post-WW2 setting, this paper applies it to a colonial context.

Prior to the First World War, the British-Indian military, a colonial institution, was charged with protecting British interests both within India and abroad. The British Indian Army, especially its Sikh and Gurkha regiments, fought in the Second Anglo-Afghan War (1878–1880), the Mahdist War (1881–1899), and the Boxer Rebellion in China (1899–1900). Indian soldiers also played a pivotal role in the expansion of the British empire, with Indian forces being stationed in Egypt to help secure British control over the Suez Canal (1882) and Afghanistan (1880). During all three Anglo-Afghan wars of 1839–42, 1878–80, and 1919, the British-Indian army played a vital role in the campaigns, especially during the bloodiest of them, the Second Anglo-Afghan War, where they served as the primary force in combat operations and provided logistical support. Soldiers of the Gurkha and Sikh regiments were the backbone of the British-Indian army during the Afghan campaigns.

During the Anglo-Egyptian and Second Anglo-Afghan Wars, the British-Indian military was an extremely professional force, demonstrated through its disciplined organisation and high levels of experience in both its officer and enlisted ranks. This paper categorises that professionalism under four points, in no particular order: competence, loyalty, high ethical standards, and discipline. The force was made up of veterans of the Second Anglo-Afghan War and the Third Anglo-Burmese War. Following the Sepoy Mutiny of 1857, the British military was reformed in order to prevent future insurrections, resulting in soldiers having greater technical skills and competency, and being paid better. That high pay lends itself to the next point, loyalty: there were two main factors behind the loyalty of Indian troops — financial incentives, and the martial races doctrine applied to military recruitment.

The British-Indian military of this time was shaped by the martial races theory, a colonial doctrine that categorized all castes as belonging to one of two categories. The "martial" category — namely Sikhs, Gurkhas, Pathans, and Rajputs — were believed to be more suited for military service due to their physical strength, perceived loyalty to the British, and levels of discipline, and were recruited with less access to education, as they were believed to be easier to control. The other, "non-martial" category comprised Marathis and Gujaratis, among others. This focus of recruitment had significant implications for the loyalty of soldiers in all conflicts, including the Anglo-Egyptian War of 1882, embedding a sense of pride and identity within the martial races that fostered a deep sense of loyalty to the British military, reinforced by the notion that they were the protectors of the Empire.

The officers and soldiers of the British-Indian army also had extremely high ethical standards. In the officer class, this was shaped by British colonial values and upbringing, and played a significant role in maintaining discipline and order — officers were trained to lead by example. This was demonstrated in the Anglo-Egyptian War of 1882 by commanders such as General Sir Gerald Graham, known for his ethical conduct toward prisoners, and Field Marshal Garnet Joseph Wolseley, who maintained strict orders against looting and unnecessary destruction of Cairo and neighbouring cities. Discipline was exhibited by both officers and soldiers, with a strict chain of command and officers directly responsible for the actions of their soldiers — Wolseley was known for maintaining the "Wolseley Ring," a group of officers entrusted with maintaining discipline and order within the force. This strict discipline was one of the key reasons the British-Indian army was able to defeat forces that severely outnumbered them.

This concurs with Janowitz's theory that prior to the First World War, the British-Indian military was mainly concerned with traditional warfare, serving the interests of the British empire in the conquest of new territory and the defense of its colonial possessions — the first phase of Janowitz's theory, in which a military is solely focused on traditional colonial warfare.

From the beginning of the First World War to India's independence on 15th August 1947, the British-Indian military transformed significantly. Originally established to defend the interests of the British Empire, it began to perform roles traditionally associated with internal governance organisations, while still fighting in foreign theatres. This was crucial due to increased demand for self-rule within India. In the years following the First World War, the Indian military was mainly involved in suppressing discontent with the British administration. Reactions within the military were mixed — many Indian soldiers and officers felt tension between their sense of duty to the British and growing nationalistic sentiment, but a combination of professionalism and fear of punishment kept soldiers from openly defying orders. For British officers, particularly senior ones, this constabulary role was viewed as a necessary part of maintaining control; while some were uncomfortable with methods employed during incidents such as the Jallianwala Bagh Massacre, they were extremely unlikely to refuse orders as a result of their training. As the 1930s progressed, the military's role as a constabulary force was increasingly questioned by both the Indian public and some within the military, reflected in events such as the 1946 Royal Indian Navy Mutiny.

During this period the army was frequently deployed to quell protests, strikes, and public unrest, sometimes through violent suppression using live ammunition and, in extreme cases, machine guns. However, this was the exception rather than the norm — the military's role was more akin to that of a police force, present to intimidate and suppress crowds and to engage in combat only when deemed necessary, as with the Salt March (1930) and the Non-Cooperation Movement. This establishes that in the years between the First and Second World Wars (1919–1939), the Indian Military played the role of a constabulary force to maintain order within India, while also remaining a traditional combat force projecting British power abroad — the second phase of Janowitz's theory, in which a military is focused both on constabulary-like engagements and on traditional combat.

By comparison, the French military in Algeria took on both constabulary and combat roles but placed far greater emphasis on direct control through violent subjugation, while the Belgian military in the Congo was deployed with a dual role but with far less emphasis on traditional combat, its main task being the suppression of internal conflict. The British-Indian military's almost equal role in both large-scale military operations and internal peacekeeping was unique among colonial militaries. This was due to several factors: the size and diversity of India, which strained the capacity of the police to handle uprisings and protests; the rise of nationalist movements requiring suppression; and the scale of India's involvement in global conflicts such as the two World Wars.

During this period the British-Indian military also underwent "Indianisation" — the transfer of administrative and leadership roles from British to Indian officers. The necessities of the First World War meant a significant number of soldiers from "non-martial" races, mainly Bengalis and South Indians, were inducted, though mainly into labor, support, and auxiliary positions; by the Second World War, significant labour shortages further relaxed the martial races principle, with many non-martial soldiers serving as combat troops. This process, and the gradual relaxation of the martial races theory, allowed Indian soldiers to gain experience in leadership, administration, and strategic planning — a transition crucial in preparing the Indian officer corps for independence, and a key factor in the success of the Indian military in Kashmir, Hyderabad, and Junagadh.

After India's independence on 15th August 1947, the British-Indian military was formally divided into the Pakistan Armed Forces and the Indian Armed Forces. In the years following partition, the Indian military primarily played the role of a constabulary force, as shown through the integration of the princely states and through maintaining order amid the disorganisation of the Indian civil government, especially in once-disputed territories such as Junagadh and Kashmir. Yet in the Indo-Pakistani War of 1947–1948, the Indian military also played the role of a traditional combat force, defending the nation's borders — challenging Janowitz's theory, which holds that a democratic state's military will evolve into a purely constabulary force. This dual role highlights the complex nature of India's post-independence security, and how critical the Indian military was to the formation of India as a state.

Much research has also been conducted into post-colonial civil-military relations in Africa. During British colonial rule, the British-Indian Army was highly organized and managed through British command, becoming a critical source of manpower for Britain; after independence, India inherited a professional military able to get the upper hand in managing civil-military relations. The colonial military system in Nigeria, by contrast, was far less centralised and fragmented, relying on a combination of local militias, British troops, and regional forces such as the Royal West African Frontier Force, with Nigerians recruited only into subordinate roles. The "Indianisation" that happened to the British-Indian Army never happened with the Nigerian force, which was a key reason Nigeria's transition to independence in 1960 was fraught with instability and political fragmentation — the military quickly got involved in politics, resulting in a coup d'état only six years later. The Indian military, by contrast, came under direct civilian control, with far less power than other postcolonial militaries of the era, such as Pakistan (coup in 1958), Egypt (1952), Burma (1962), and Ghana (1966).

Following the Second World War, non-martial-race regiments were disbanded, and the Indian National Congress criticised the martial races recruitment policy as restrictive and discriminatory. In February 1949, the Indian government abolished the official application of martial race principles in recruitment, though it continued to be applied informally — to this day, the backbone of the Indian Army remains mainly Sikhs, Dogras, Jats, and Rajputs, the same groups identified by the British as "martial races."

## Case Studies — The First Kashmir War, Operation Polo, and the Military Takeover of Junagadh

### Case Study 1 — The First Kashmir War (1947–1948)

The First Kashmir War is one of the most prominent examples of the Indian Army's role in the integration of the princely states and the formation of India as a state. The Indian Army's intervention against the invasion of Kashmir by tribal militias was immediate and critical to ensuring the internal security of the state, and by extension, that of the nation, since the invasion — supported by Pakistan — threatened Kashmir's sovereignty and could have led to its annexation by Pakistan. The rapid mobilisation of the Indian army halted the advance of tribal militias, ensuring that the strategically important region remained under India's control, given Kashmir's geographical significance and proximity to Pakistan and China. Without this action, Kashmir's fall to Pakistan would have altered the regional balance of power, exposed India's northern borders to military threats from both Pakistan and China, and jeopardized India's control over vital water resources such as the Indus Water System.

The Kashmir War was also a test of whether India could protect its territorial integrity. India's prompt military response, combined with its diplomatic move to seek United Nations intervention while maintaining a strong military presence, demonstrated both military capability and organizational strength to the undecided princely states and the Indian population, strengthening both India's international standing and its civilian government's domestic standing.

Finally, according to scholars such as P.R. Chari and Anit Mukherjee, while military commanders played a vital role in the operational and strategic execution of military tasks, final decisions regarding military operations and diplomacy rested in the hands of civilian leadership. Chari states that "the political culture developed within India has crystallised civil-military relations within a definite pattern." This became evident during the First Kashmir War, where final decisions were always made by Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru, Deputy Prime Minister Vallabhbhai Patel, and V.P. Menon — including the decision to seek international intervention (despite the military's preference for continued engagement) and the decision to deploy forces, taken by Nehru and Patel on the advice of Lord Mountbatten and Menon, only after the Maharaja had signed the instrument of accession, and against the advice of senior military leaders. Nevertheless, military leaders such as Major General Thimayya played major roles in the strategic execution of operations. Overall, the Kashmir War was more than a military conflict — it was a significant period in the formation of India as a state, shaping the course of Indian and international history and placing India on the world stage by proving both its democratic capabilities and its military might.

### Case Study 2 — Operation Polo (Hyderabad)

During Operation Polo in Hyderabad, the military was not the state itself, but rather a tool of the state used to integrate the princely states — an example of the Indian military acting as a constabulary force in accordance with Janowitz's theory. By late 1948, almost all of the 500+ princely states had acceded to either India or Pakistan, but Hyderabad, at the centre of the country and in the midst of a communist rebellion (the Telangana Rebellion), was holding out. Deputy Prime Minister Vallabhbhai Patel stated that "if Hyderabad was not integrated, it would become a cancer in the belly of India." The rebellion, with its abolition of forced labour and illegal eviction and return of illegally seized lands, had strong peasant support and was likely to have succeeded without the Indian military's "police action." With diplomatic efforts failing, Patel and Nehru authorised military intervention. Though operational details were planned by military commanders, the strategic direction was provided by Patel and Nehru, setting a precedent for civilian control over future operations, including the Second Kashmir War and the Sino-Indian War of 1962.

Operation Polo — dubbed a "police action" — exemplified the military functioning not as a traditional force, but as a constabulary one, moving away from the traditional fighting role it had played in Kashmir. Without the integration of Hyderabad, India would not have become a fully formed state, given the risk of constant internal conflict and violence; the Indian military's swift and uncompromising action played a major role in preventing that outcome.

### Case Study 3 — Military Takeover of Junagadh

Junagadh offers an additional example of military intervention used to integrate the princely states. At the time, Junagadh had a majority Hindu population (~85%) and a Muslim Nawab (ruler); given its proximity to the present-day Indo-Pakistan border, it had the potential to accede to either nation, unlike Hyderabad. Following partition, the Nawab acceded to Pakistan, a decision that led to unrest among the Hindu majority, who called for integration with India. The Indian government first attempted diplomatic means, with Patel negotiating with the Nawab to reconsider; when the Nawab instead fled to Pakistan in September 1947, and with Pakistan claiming Junagadh while its people wanted to accede to India, Patel and Nehru took military action before the situation spiralled out of control — preventing a war similar to Kashmir, with no obvious victory guaranteed for Indian forces. As in Kashmir and Hyderabad, senior civilian leadership made the final decision to act, with military officers planning the operational details.

The Indian army played the role of a purely constabulary force during its occupation of Junagadh: it did not engage in combat, but advanced with little resistance and occupied strategic locations — including Junagadh's airport and towns such as Junagadh city, Keshod, and Mangrol — to assert control and restore order, allowing the people to voice their desire for integration without fear of reprisal from the Nawab's forces. The Indian government then called for a plebiscite, held under its supervision, in which more than 90% of the population voted to join India. The Indian military's role in Junagadh showcased India's resolve not to tolerate areas of unresolved conflict, and demonstrated that India could combine military force with diplomacy and democratic process — aligning with India's broader principles of non-aggression and peaceful resolution wherever possible.

## Generalisations from the Case Studies

Upon close examination of the case studies, this paper draws two generalizations about India and its military. First, in all three case studies, the military was ultimately under the control of civilian leadership, and despite the need for military force, civilian leaders combined military intervention with democratic and diplomatic process to the best of their efforts — demonstrated by the Junagadh plebiscite, and by the numerous diplomatic attempts made by Patel and Nehru in Hyderabad and Kashmir before military action was taken.

Second, the use of military force was paramount in securing India's territorial integrity and ensuring national security, illustrating how military intervention, though typically a last resort, was sometimes necessary to ensure the stability of the Indian state. In Kashmir, military force was critical in defending the region against the Pakistani-backed invasion, which threatened not only Kashmir's independence but the territorial integrity and stability of the Indian state as a whole — and was deployed only after diplomacy had failed. In Hyderabad, given the risk of a communist state with a fundamentally different ideology persisting within India's borders, military operation was necessary to secure India's territorial integrity and national security, again as a last resort. In Junagadh, though the military did not engage in combat, its initial intervention was vital in ensuring Pakistan did not send military force to back its claims — avoiding a drawn-out conflict similar to Kashmir that would have taken vital resources from the rest of the country.

## Conclusion

As mentioned in the introduction, the Indian army, having evolved from a force focused on traditional warfare to a constabulary force, was instrumental in the integration of the princely states and the formation of India as a state. As shown in the case studies, a strong military in reserve allowed the Indian civilian government to integrate Junagadh, Kashmir, and Hyderabad into the Indian union, without which India would not have become a fully formed state — whether through the loss of Kashmir to Pakistani control, the persistence of a communist Hyderabad in conflict with the rest of India, or a war over Junagadh similar to Kashmir.

Although many argue that the Indian military did not have a major role in India's formation as a state due to the civilian government's primacy over the military, without the Indian military, India's territorial integrity and sovereignty would have been significantly compromised. Whilst civilian leadership such as Nehru and Patel were vital to both the integration of the princely states and the formation of India, the military's contribution in these two areas was indispensable.

This has major implications for modern-day India, demonstrating the importance of a balanced relationship between civilian leadership and the military, and the need for coordination between the civilian government and the armed forces on national security concerns. The examples of Kashmir, Hyderabad, and Junagadh indicate that while diplomacy and democratic processes should always be the priority, military intervention may become necessary. In the present day, India's territorial disputes with both Pakistan and China continue to tread the fine line between diplomacy and military intervention — the lessons learned from operations in India's formative years continue to influence modern-day military strategy, as India remains committed to diplomatic measures while maintaining a strong military capable of responding to any threat, whether internal or external.
