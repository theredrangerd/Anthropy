Confucianism, a set of philosophical principles that are practiced primarily in East Asia, continues to shape the familial structures of modern Singapore, mainly through the values of filial piety (孝, xiao) and social harmony (和谐, héxié). This paper investigates how these Confucian principles influence family-based mental health interventions in Singapore, where mental health struggles are rising, yet remain stigmatized by society. While previous studies have explored the influence of Confucian ethics on healthcare behaviors in East Asian societies, few have examined how these principles affect mental health support within Singapore's unique multicultural and policy-driven context.

Drawing on existing literature and supported by qualitative and quantitative studies, this research finds that filial piety fosters emotional suppression by reinforcing hierarchical familial structures, which deters individuals from seeking professional help. Similarly, the cultural imperative to maintain social harmony discourages open dialogue around mental illness, as this disclosure is often perceived as a threat to family reputation. While some reinterpretations of Confucian harmony that may be more liberal have been shown to facilitate collective healing in families, these cases are rare. Overall, this study concludes that the traditional interpretations of Confucian principles contribute to the silence around mental health struggles and affect the effectiveness of family-based interventions. It argues that to improve mental health outcomes, interventions have to engage with these cultural frameworks directly, coming up with culturally sensitive approaches that work within Confucian-influenced families.

## Introduction

Confucianism is a philosophical and ethical system that has shaped East Asian societies for over two millennia. Over time, Confucian values spread throughout East Asia, heavily influencing the cultures of countries such as China, Korea, Japan, and Vietnam. In Singapore, these values were transmitted primarily through its ethnic Chinese majority, where Confucian ideals — especially those emphasizing filial piety and social harmony — continue to inform societal attitudes and family structures today. At the core of Confucianism is the belief that human relationships, particularly within the family, serve as the foundation for a stable and well-ordered society. Today, Confucian ideals remain deeply ingrained in Singaporean society, influencing familial relationships and healthcare decisions.

Family-based mental health interventions in Singapore involve collective decision-making and emphasize family cohesion. These interventions include family therapy or educational initiatives designed to improve communication and conflict resolution within the family. As stated by Deputy Prime Minister and Minister for Finance Lawrence Wong during a parliamentary motion on February 7, 2024, mental health challenges are increasingly recognized as a public health concern in Singapore. Yet, stigma and cultural barriers prevent individuals from seeking appropriate care.

While there is existing research on the effects of Confucianism on health behaviors, few scholars have examined how Confucian principles, in general, and the concept of filial piety, in particular, inform family-based mental health interventions in Singapore. Research like Fan's (2007) study looks at Confucian medical ethics and how familial obligation or respect for authority influences patient autonomy and treatment compliance. Kim et al. (2020) examine family decision-making around healthcare in an East Asian context, finding that Confucian values tend to privilege collective functioning over individual mental health needs, out of fear of "losing face." Yang & Kleinman (2008) provide insight into how traditional Chinese cultural values shape the process and outcomes of psychiatric care. However, there remains a gap in the literature specifically addressing how these dynamics play out within Singapore's multicultural and policy-driven mental health landscape — a gap this paper aims to fill.

This study aims to build on these findings by analyzing specifically how Confucian principles interact with mental health interventions in Singapore. The influence of Confucian philosophy, particularly its emphasis on filial piety, has negative effects on family-based mental health interventions and outcomes in Singapore. By examining Confucianism's role in shaping mental health interventions, researchers and policymakers can better address cultural barriers that prevent effective mental health care.

## Filial Piety

The Five Pillars of Confucianism are the principles at the core of Confucianism, which include Ren (仁, benevolence and compassion), Yi (义, righteousness and justice), Li (礼, propriety and social norms), Zhi (智, wisdom and knowledge), and Xin (信, integrity and trustworthiness). These are the common values of social control and moral norms that would result in the cultivation of filial piety (孝, xiao) in Confucian ethics, where the emphasis is on obedience and the duty towards parents and ancestors.

These historical values would further trace back to the teachings of Confucius (551–479 BCE), which sought to maintain a social order around virtue and moral values. In the Analects (论语), a collection of sayings and ideas of Chinese philosopher and politician Confucius, he points out the hierarchical conditionality as well as mutuality in relationships when he states that "A ruler should govern his people with virtue and rituals, and they will develop a sense of shame and become upright" (Analects, 2:3).

This explains how Confucian principles, particularly Li, serve as a means for social control. When individuals consistently practice propriety and moral education, they also internalize their roles within society, reinforcing the hierarchical structures that sustain the filial piety principle. In this sense, control is not imposed coercively but rather cultivated through strict adherence to the tradition which Confucius sets up himself.

Filial piety, as an extension of these core values, is explicitly addressed in another Analects passage: "Filial piety does not mean merely providing for one's parents; true filial piety lies in bringing them honor and ensuring their actions align with virtue" (Analects, 2:7). The traditional interpretation clarifies that the Xiao principle (filial piety) is not only about obedience but also about moral responsibility. This makes sure that an individual's family maintains a virtuous reputation.

One of the most well-known quotations from the Analects that illustrates the definition of filial piety is: "The Master said, 'Filial piety nowadays means being able to support one's parents. But dogs and horses are also able to do this. If there is no reverence, wherein lies the difference?'" (Analects 2.7). In the context of mental health interventions in Singapore, this philosophical foundation complicates the approaches to care. Mental health struggles are under-communicated or dismissed to fulfill perceived filial duties under this principle. It requires the prioritization of family needs over personal desires. Filial piety extends beyond individual relationships to influence social expectations, reinforcing a sense of duty toward elders and shaping behaviors in various aspects of life — one of them being mental health interventions.

Studies have shown, for instance, that families have resisted mental health treatments that challenge hierarchical structures, or ask children to talk about personal struggles to one another, out of concern that this could demand family harmony or even upset elders within that hierarchy.

Moreover, research from the National Library of Medicine (Yu et al.) examined how Confucian values affect caregiver stress among families of individuals with severe mental illnesses. The study surveyed 200 caregivers in East Asian communities and found that Ren (仁, benevolence) and Yi (义, righteousness) can also increase psychological distress by creating expectations of self-sacrifice. Caregivers, therefore, prioritized duty over self-care, leading to higher rates of emotional exhaustion among the caregivers. The study's findings show very clearly that while Confucianism builds strong support networks, its emphasis on filial piety can lead to caregiver burnout and also result in reluctance to seek external mental health assistance. This principle can encourage emotional suppression and conformity to traditional family values, subjecting caregivers to enormous stressors and potentially placing them at risk of caregiver burden and declining physical and mental health.

Despite the reported positive correlation between authoritarian filial piety and mental health, there is reason to believe that it will also reinforce harmful behavioral patterns in families where children struggle with mental health conditions. Yeh (2018) explains that parental expectations rooted in authoritarian filial piety will delegitimize children's mental health concerns, framing them as a lack of discipline rather than a legitimate medical issue. Furthermore, Kwan et al. (2021) show that the emphasis on hierarchy and obedience prevents children from openly discussing mental distress, leading to underutilization of mental health services.

Although Leck (2023) found a positive correlation between both reciprocal and authoritarian filial piety and self-reported mental well-being, in Confucian-influenced societies like Singapore, individuals equate adherence to filial duty with virtue or satisfaction, even if this obedience comes at the expense of their own psychological needs. The study also does not sufficiently examine how these dynamics play out in families experiencing severe or chronic mental illness, where the stakes of emotional suppression and caregiver strain are far more acute.

The way filial piety is interpreted in societies like Singapore brings about mental and emotional suppression and makes it extremely hard for individuals to stand up for themselves and seek help. Because there is a perceived duty to parents, children will do everything in their power to maintain peace in the relationship. Worried that opening up about emotional struggles will ruin that balance, they stop, and therefore don't seek help.

## Social Harmony

Social harmony, or 和谐 (héxié), is another core principle of Confucianism. It emphasizes the importance of balance as well as the maintenance of internal order within families and society. While filial piety (孝, xiao) governs hierarchical roles and duties within familial relationships, social harmony is the overarching principle that guides how those roles are carried out in the real world, prioritizing collective well-being over individual expression. According to Confucius, the "superior man seeks harmony, not conformity" (Analects, 13:23). This statement is traditionally interpreted to mean that while differences exist, open conflict should be avoided to sustain balance. As illustrated by Analects 8:2, "Respectfulness, without the rules of propriety (Li), becomes tiresome; carefulness, without the rules of propriety, becomes timidity; courage, without the rules of propriety, becomes insubordination; straightforwardness, without the rules of propriety, becomes rudeness."

Confucianism conceptualizes mental illness not as a psychological or biological condition, but as a disruption of social order. As such, individuals suffering from mental illnesses in these societies are perceived as having failed to fulfill their social role in maintaining harmony within the family or their community. Chow et al. (2010) highlight this dynamic in their study on Chinese lay theories of mental illness, noting that mental illness is frequently interpreted as a moral failing or character flaw. Within Confucian cultures, people internalize this stigma and view their suffering as a personal weakness that dishonors their family, disincentivizing help-seeking behaviors from people who need help. This stigma is particularly prevalent in family contexts where open expression of distress is discouraged for fear of "causing disharmony." Participants in Chow et al.'s study expressed a reluctance to disclose emotional problems, scared they would burden family members with their problems. In these environments, the social harmony principle implicitly encourages emotional suppression and silence, even in cases where help is necessary.

In Singapore, where Confucian values shape social and familial expectations, the value of harmony can have both constraining and enabling effects on mental health interventions. Badanta et al. (2022) concluded that Confucian values significantly influence mental health behaviors, leading families to prioritize internal problem-solving over professional consultation. In their scoping review analysing 40 studies to assess the influence of Confucian values on health behaviors and medical decisions, they found that families resist psychiatric services unless necessary, preferring to manage psychological issues privately to avoid exposing familial "disharmony" to outsiders. The nature of this resistance is rooted in Confucian principles that emphasize family centrality and social harmony. Families perceive seeking external psychiatric help as a potential source of "disharmony," leading them to handle mental health issues privately. This approach aims to protect the family's public image and adhere to cultural expectations of self-reliance and internal resolution. This is particularly concerning in family-based interventions, where open communication is crucial for diagnosis and support. The pressure to uphold harmony can prevent individuals, particularly children who are at the bottom of the familial hierarchy, from voicing personal struggles, especially when these issues challenge parental authority.

Yet, not all families influenced by Confucian values experience social harmony as a barrier to mental health care in the same way. In a qualitative study conducted in Singapore, Lim and Lim (2016) explored how families engaged with systemic psychotherapy and found that when social harmony was understood not as avoidance of conflict but as active collaboration, it facilitated meaningful engagement with therapeutic processes. In these families, the value of upholding social harmony motivated participants to support one another's healing for the sake of preserving relational unity. Mental health professionals working in these contexts found that aligning therapeutic goals with the value of harmony — such as reframing treatment as a means of restoring family balance instead of impeding it — improved receptiveness to mental health services.

The case described in Lim and Lim (2016) is special, not the norm, because it reflects a reinterpretation of Confucian harmony that is less traditional and shaped by external influences like education and more modern cultural norms. In traditional Confucian ethics, as illustrated by Analects 1:12, "If the ruler is himself correct, all will go well even though he does not give orders; but if he himself is not correct, even though he gives orders, they will not be obeyed." Social harmony is often preserved through emotional restraint to suppress conflict, especially within hierarchical family systems. What makes the families in Lim and Lim's study different is that they redefined harmony not as the absence of conflict, but as the active presence of cooperation and mutual care. This is a progressive application of Confucian principles. Such positive outcomes, therefore, are contingent on a reinterpretation of harmony that allows for open expression within respectful boundaries. Without this reinterpretation, social harmony as a principle of Confucianism remains detrimental to individuals with mental health struggles, because it can discourage the openness needed for effective mental health interventions.

## Conclusion

This paper has argued that while Confucianism plays a foundational role in shaping social and familial norms in Singapore, its principles, particularly filial piety (孝) and social harmony (和谐), ultimately have a detrimental impact on family-based mental health interventions. Confucianism's emphasis on hierarchical obligation and the preservation of relational stability reinforces environments where mental illness is stigmatized and, therefore, professional help-seeking is discouraged.

Filial piety imposes strict expectations of obedience and sacrifice, often making individuals silence their emotional struggles out of fear of disrupting the family reputation. Likewise, social harmony, when interpreted as the avoidance of conflict and the prioritization of external appearances, reinforces the idea that psychological distress should be handled privately, if not concealed entirely. Families, therefore, choose internal problem-solving over professional consultation, not because of a lack of need, but because Confucian ideals make external intervention feel culturally inappropriate.

Though isolated cases, such as those in Lim and Lim's (2016) study, show that Confucian values can be reinterpreted to support collective healing, such examples are exceptions, not the norm. The broader reality coming from traditional texts like the Analects is that filial piety and social harmony hinder open communication and access to mental health resources, especially for those who need it most.

Therefore, to improve mental health outcomes in Singapore, interventions must go beyond clinical models and directly engage with the cultural frameworks that perpetuate emotional suppression and stigma. Recognizing how Confucianism shapes resistance to mental health care is a necessary step toward helping those who are suffering, which continues to persist behind the façade of family harmony and duty.
