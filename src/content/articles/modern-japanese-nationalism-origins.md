The resurgent domestic popularity of the Japanese right-wing nationalist ideology in its local political landscape since the late 1990s can be seen as a manifestation of both economic dissatisfaction that existed within the population, and the pressure that originated from the escalating domestic frustration and tension between Japan and its East Asian neighbours including China and North Korea. The latter could be deemed more immediate due to the responsive nature and the potential national security risks for the country. Yet the primary manifestation, which is the united national identity and the desire for a strong nation, remains ultimately less tangible to the domestic electorate. This is due to the fact that geopolitical concerns are fundamentally political rhetoric in the media, while economic stagnation directly and disproportionately impacts the citizens. Concurrently, there are other supplementary influences, such as the revisionist approach to education, as well as the negative redirection of xenophobic rhetoric and populist narratives in media towards neighbouring states after their rapid development in the 21st century. Yet ultimately, the pivotal element of this debate is still the realist concern of economic burdens, which fulfils the crucial threshold of relevance and tangibility for the majority of the Japanese population. Which is due to its high degree of tangibility and direct impact on the living standards of the Japanese population, which is the closest, and arguably the most important determinant of public opinion.

## Introduction

In recent decades, the global political landscape has witnessed a pronounced shift toward the revisionist, far-right alternative as compared to the more common pacifist, liberal political approach. As a country with deep-rooted connections to the establishment of nationalism and ultra-nationalism in the form of militarism, Japan acts as one of the leading figures in this process from the late 19th century up till date. This trend is generally interpreted as a form of modern reconstruction of the far-right ideology that has existed in Japan since the Meiji era (1868-1912). Although the trend itself and the impact are undeniably profound, existing literature suffers from a critical deficit: it rarely offers an explicit comparison between the factors that are widely recognised as main contributors to the shift. This paper aims to address this by tracing and evaluating existing arguments in academia, focusing on extracting the key comparison between the two largest facilitators to the change seen in the current society. These examine the economic factors seen in the widespread dissatisfaction of its people and the rising pressure regarding both tension in foreign relations and domestic political turmoil. The importance of cause in the field of politics is significant in a unique analytical lens, as the evaluation of these often becomes the foundation of theories such as the International Relations theories, which interprets societal systems and provide insights on patterns that helps us to understand the field of deontology further.

Many existing studies have extensively analysed the causal mechanisms behind this change, which is widely identified as a mixture of political pressure, domestic discontent regarding diplomatic policies and economic strategies. Current literature categorizes these elements often at the same level, and are often discussed separately. While some papers prioritise on diplomatic issues, others often highlight the structural economic stagnation, high deflationary pressure, and declining real wages in Japan. This created an active academic debate on the primary driver that is unsettled, while having it being fundamental in understanding states that are capable of such political shifts.

For instance, sovereignty disputes in the South China Sea between China and Japan, such as the Senkaku Island incident, are highlighted to prove the growing presence (3, 10), and projected as evidence of Chinese aggression by scholars. This is then utilised to justify the necessity of Japan having to revise its pacifist security posture, notably Article Nine, due to their geographical approximate positions and historical legacies. The disputes are utilised as external threats, which urges the Japanese population to unify under the same umbrella of nationalism that is capable of protecting the state. This then branches into two aspects regarding the role of the USA. Some argue that Japan must further increase the extent of its collaboration with the USA as a robust, primary source of military deterrence to potential threats of Japan's sovereignty (1, 5), while others suggested that as the USA is withdrawing from the global hegemon role and shifting into an isolationist actor, Japan is much more willing to develop its own autonomous military capabilities to counteract potential regional aggression from its Asian neighbours such as from China and North Korea (1, 12, 13). This friction is further illustrated in that although Japan was a large global donor of capital, its international recognition is poor, resulting in significant domestic backlashes against the checkbook diplomacy to gain recognition, and ultimately leading to Japan decreasing its presence in international institutions such as the Korean-led Northeast Asia Peace and Cooperation Initiative (NAPCI) that aims to establish order (11, 12). Nationalists often weaponise the public disapproval of the spending pattern, approaching it as an opportunity cost for domestic development, further fueling the discontent on the state's economic policies.

Domestic factors are another recurring concept in existing academia. For example, a stance amongst Japanese citizens argue that since the end of World War Two, the perceived, overly apologetic attitude of the postwar Japanese government undermined the pride of Japan as a country with its unique and significant cultural significance. This issue is exacerbated by the demographic of the Japanese population, as the generation that experienced the terror of war has mostly passed, and the younger generation often lacks the fear and reverence of war but instead glorified the martial traditions. This, and other factors such as xenophobia against Chinese and Koreans provided the base for nationalistic sentiments to manifest at a large scale amongst the Japanese populace. This is further complemented by the lowering of voting age in the Abe administration, a far-right nationalist politician that served as Japan's Prime Minister famously from 2012 to 2020 (3, 4, 11). This is accompanied by the structural revisionist change in action, such as the revision of education materials and increasing frequency of leaders' official visits to the Yasukuni Shrine, in which Class A war criminals from World War Two are honoured. All of which are diplomatically disadvantageous, but morally significant as a fuel to the nationalistic sentiments in the Japanese population (4, 11).

Last, the economic failure of Japan in the past few decades was also frequently discussed, and can be summarized to a few elements. For instance, some scholars noticed that the economic structure of Japan itself is highly fragile and susceptible to external impacts, namely the significant portion of workers in small firms which are immensely protected, subsidized, yet is eminently unproductive and uncompetitive globally (3). In addition, the culture of seniority being more weighty than merit also suggested that the economy is unlikely to be flexible in response to the current economic environment (8). This is then expanded by many scholars to explain the economic failure of Japan, with the most evident example being the "Lost Decade" and the disastrous impact of the 2008 world economic crisis, all of which leading to the significant downfall of Japan's economy, resulting in a large gap between now and the golden era of Japan when it was the second largest economy in the world. It is argued that both the reducing living standards and the relative deprivation of economic power contribute to the increasing nationalistic sentiments, which politicians utilised to attract voters with reforms such as the "Abenomics", as well as the psychological returns for the lost financial wages through embracing nationalistic cultures (1, 3, 4, 8, 15).

While all of the existing studies provide valuable context to the issue, gaps still exist in terms of the evaluation of these causes. In most of the research, all the elements are given equal weight as to their contribution to large-scale nationalism. Although it is true that they are all valid contributions, the fragmented evaluation and analysis of separate parts can be potentially misleading as to how the wave grew to such an extent and scale. By reorganising the two largest components of all the causes, it becomes possible to discern how the Japanese population perceived these issues differently. The nuance in the individual agency aims to offer a more holistic analysis of the demographics, which could potentially provide the foundation for the evaluation of wider political case studies that relates to the development of nationalism.

## The Nationalist Ideal in Japanese Politics

In the late 20th and early 21st centuries, political turmoil seemed to be the defining characteristic of Japanese politics in both demographical and diplomatic aspects, leading to large public discourse. This eventually developed into sentiments of nationalism, especially amongst the youth of the time. While frequently cited in academic literature, the tangible impact of these political shifts on the average citizen remains a subject of debate. This section evaluates both domestic and external factors in Japan's political landscape with focus on three key criterias: scale, profundity and political response. By applying these criteria, we will be able to see the significant role of politics in daily life. While these political rhetoric may sound compelling and high on scale, the relative tangibility and degree of impact is largely subservient to economic factors.

### The Revision of Historical Remnants

The potential for Japanese large-scale nationalistic development is undeniable in Japan, as nationalism has persisted since the late Tokugawa era (1600-1868), and later developing into a nascent version of the modern one in the Meiji era that followed. The original form of nationalism aimed to develop Japan as a whole, after the emperor saw the large disparity between Japan and Western nations due to their industrial revolutions and development of technologies. After generations of effort to develop, the outcome was that Japan at the time prospered and became one of the strongest nations in Asia. This very much fed into the ego and pride of the Japanese populace, and fueled the idea that Japan as a country held superiority over other lesser developed nations. One example of this would be the concept of the "Great East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere" during the Second World War, in which Japan sees itself as the leading figure to bring up its undeveloped neighbours. One other crucial aspect of the original ideology is that Japan is a sacred country that is protected by the sun goddess Amaterasu with the emperor being the descendant of the goddess herself, which further enhances the idea that Japanese people are uniquely superior compared to all others. (Wikipedia, Japanese Nationalism) The implication here is that the ideology of nationalism and Japanese superiority is deeply integrated into the education of new generations more or less as part of the country's identity. This is seen in the school enforced cultural activities and the curriculum that often had an emphasis on past economic or military glories. This inherited ideology, alongside the "Japanese miracle" period from the 1960s to the 1980s together crafted the picture of a strong and powerful nation that is independent. The key inference here is that the divine identity made nationalism in Japan especially convincing, as a fundamental shared belief that is part of the nation's culture itself. (Matthews, 2003)

Still, the Japanese population has demonstrated a susceptibility to the populist narrative of reclaiming lost national prestige after militarism in Japan was eradicated by the world order after World War Two. Parallels can be drawn from the past with the present, as one of the key actions of the Abe administration was to revise the school curriculum, utilising it as a sanitized national rhetoric. One example would be references to "Comfort Women", which refers to the captured women and girls that are forced into sexual slavery by the imperial army during the Second World War. This revisionist approach largely fostered the growth of emotions such as Japanese pride and superiority, all of which having a large potential of developing into nationalism. (Koichi, 2016) Patriotic beliefs, altered history, and xenophobic attitudes integrated as parts of education can be extremely penetrating and persuasive to the younger generation, who typically lacks exposure to a diverse range of perspectives from the international field due to Japan's generally isolationist approach in cultural matters and media. A voter base at the scale of a generation with patriotic and xenophobic deeply implanted in their minds offers the most potential for nationalism to grow in the country.

### The International Stage, Apologetic Diplomacy, and Changing Perceptions

During the late 20th and up until the 21st century, Japan was a leading donor on the world stage, and this even included an ODA (Official Development Assistance) investment in China, in which Japanese people originally took pride as a means to shape a prosperous Asia. This was a very substantial support, as the investment allowed the secondary sector in China to rapidly develop, which then boosted the Asian economy as a whole due to the increase in trade. (Matthews, 2003) In addition, their contributions in international institutions such as the UN and the World Bank are extensive as well, this includes being the second-largest capital supporter of The World Bank. However, the lack of Japanese figures in high positions of these institutions, and the lack of recognition from China and Korea regarding their apologies for wartime crimes suggested hinted at the fact that their financial contribution yielded negligible diplomatic returns. This situation is more evident in the 1990 Gulf War against Iraq, in which Japan had sent US$13 billion worth of support, but received no recognition from Kuwait (Wallace, 2016) other than criticisms regarding Article Nine in the Japanese constitution, which renounces Japan's right of war and maintaining military forces. The disparity between contribution and influence was a major factor in Japanese people not recognising the ideals of pacifism which the rest of the world celebrates.

The idea here is that a large portion of Japanese people are unsatisfied with the return they receive from their financial investments for diplomacy. At this stage, the Japanese population's perception of soft-power diplomacy became that it failed in achieving the aim of regaining international recognition. It is then natural that some pivoted to the harder stance of nationalism that promoted military strength. (Wallace, 2016)

This coincided with the "apologetic" diplomatic strategy the Japanese government used towards China and Korea for its war crimes, seen in leaders avoiding official visits to the Yasukuni Shrine to abstain from diplomatic friction. The strategy is widely opposed by the young generation in the 21st century, as they perceived it as actively eroding the national identity, which, as mentioned earlier, was a crucial part of their education. (Matthews, 2003) This overall resentment towards the unbalanced international recognition of Japan acted as the primary catalyst for the nationalist sentiments in Japan, as it didn't represent any specific individuals or groups, but it represented the Japanese identity they found pride in. This overarching suppression of Japan suggests that the scale of political discontent not only exists, but exists at a large scale in Japan. This is significant because as mentioned above, the Japanese identity is universal across the demographic apart from policies, and the subconscious distinction between the two made it extremely convincing even to the part of the demographic that is uninterested in politics.

In reviewing the policy of Koizumi and Abe, a key similarity is that they aimed to restore Japan's national prestige and sovereign stature instead of focusing on more desperate issues such as inflation or fewer innovations. This distinction reveals the key to their successful campaigns, which is that in such times of widespread dissatisfaction, a unified identity addresses a widespread desire for national cohesion. The prerequisite for this campaign strategy to work is the overwhelming scale of people who share similar thoughts, thus proving the argument that scale of political resentment was a key in the uprise of visible nationalism.

### Sovereignty, Threats, and Aggressors

Beyond the scale of the impact, profundity of political threats are equally appealing, mainly seen in the increasing tension between Japan and external actors. After World War Two, Japan, having been a loyal ally to the USA, acted as a stabilizing force in Asia as USA's outreach station. Japan has long relied on the bilateral defence relationship with the USA, but when North Korea started to conduct their nuclear tests in 2006, the domestic attitude towards the reliance started to change. The Japanese public grew increasingly intolerant of the persistent existential threat, and the best way for Japan to avoid the risk at the time was nothing other than the direct intervention of the US to eradicate any nuclear device in North Korea. (Atanassova-Cornelis, 2014) However, the US decided to not engage directly and instead adopted more isolationist policies towards them. The steep contrast between the potential harm to Japan and the inaction of the world resulted in a widespread strategic anxiety amongst Japanese citizens and the government, further pushing them towards the goal of remilitarising through the revision of Article Nine to protect Japan. This coincided with the divergence of US interest from Japanese interests, leading to more questioning in the security guarantee. For instance, In a 2003 poll by Asahi Shimbun, 60% of Japanese citizens thought the US led Iraq war was not justified. In addition, some also started to question the impregnability of the US after the event of September 11th, which further increased the necessity of an independent force of Japan. (Matthews, 2003) To make clear, Article 9 became part of the Japanese constitution following their defeat in the second world war, and states that Japan must renounce war as a sovereign right, and may not have a standing army with the potential to maintain war potential. Although the intention of North Korea remains unclear, their ability to destroy Japan is severe enough for its people to realise that the country must be able to defend itself instead of relying on external actors for security. (Woodward, 2017) Although the collaboration between Japan and the USA remained tighter than ever still, the public fear of Nuclear threat provided a sufficient rationale for the Japanese population to vote for the nationalistic ideals that promised protection. This is exceptionally powerful as political rhetoric, as the perceived existential threat is much stronger compared to other forms of diplomatic issues in the minds of average citizens. When the threat itself is intolerable, it is able to bypass certain political logic and catalyse the growing of more extreme forms of nationalism that is perceived as capable of securing the state. (Berkofsky, 2017)

Apart from the threat of North Korea, tangible events also played a crucial part in strengthening Japan's pivot towards rearmament and support for nationalism. The contested administrative history of the Senkaku Island dispute between Japan and China is the most well-known due to the large media coverage and lasting effects internationally. The island is located between the Okinawa island of Japan and Taiwan. The Island was supposed to be given back to China after Japan's defeat in the war, but the US, who held the island, gave Japan the administrative right of the island. In 2010, Japanese coastal guard captured a Chinese trawler in the water, and threatened to prosecute it under the charge of incursion into the Japanese Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ). The ruling party, the Democratic Party of Japan (DPJ) released the trawler, and soon announced a large joint military exercise with the US in Japanese waters to deter China from further actions. The DPJ's softer approach towards China created a domestic backlash, which established the contrast between it and the LDP's stronger image and nationalistic narratives. (Woodward, 2017)

A significant escalation occurred in 2012, when Ishihara, the nationalist governor of Tokyo urged the Japanese government to buy the land deed of the island for resource extraction without notifying China. Subsequently, Chinese surveillance and warships are constantly seen in the surrounding areas, and the widespread anti-Japan protest rose across 100 Chinese cities, coinciding with the boycott of the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and World Bank meetings in the same year. As a result, tourism and export plummeted for Japan, yet Abe took a hardline pro-Japan stance, and used this to further justify the military presence of the US in Japan, increasing the military budget, and further revision of Article 9. Similar events happened to the Dokdo Island of South Korea, which also led to a series of boycotts and overall negative sentiments against Japan. The Japanese response here is not to de-escalate the events to avoid consequences, but to utilise the negative perceptions of China and North Korea to achieve their own goal of revising Article 9. This showed that the strategic aim for the responses was not to avoid any practical loss, but to strengthen the xenophobic perspective amongst the Japanese population. This is then utilised to achieve nationalist aims in the end, suggesting that it is nationalism that remains at the core of government's diplomatic responses instead of truly beneficiary incentives. (Woodward, 2017)

Those developments demonstrated that Japanese people's concerns about foreign aggression are empirically grounded. The reputation of Japan amongst its neighbours has traditionally been negative for historical causes and practical reasons like the incidents, while Japanese people find pride in them. As a result, the frequent "offences" by China is seen as active aggression against the rights and sovereignty of Japan as a country. This largely contrasted with the pacifist diplomacy China adopted in the past, and this also made Japan realise that China is starting to achieve strategic parity with them militarily. (Atanassova-Cornelis, 2014) The overall negative impression received by the Japanese population together with the extensive government propaganda provided the necessary drive for citizens to take a firmer stance against Japan's Asian neighbours in matters such as foreign policies. The negative sentiment then evolved into xenophobia in some instances, and support for nationalistic beliefs in others. In the 21st century, sales of patriotic movies and magazines skyrocketed in Japan. For instance, a film telling the story of Kamikaze pilots has a place in the top ten most successful films of all time (Wallace, 2016).

This opposition also coincided with the rapid development of China in the early 21st century in nearly all aspects, notably military technology like missiles, innovations in the internet field, and its economy, to the extent that Japan is being overtaken. The contrast between adversaries growing and Japan itself staying stagnant was considered even more serious in the younger generation that believed in the Japanese glory. (Atanassova-Cornelis, 2014) The buildup of fear and anxiety rose as an almost inevitable consequence of a multitude of reasons, including the perceived retrenchment of the US security guarantee, China rising in military and showing a clear intent of aggression, compounded by Japan's diminishing influence on the world stage. Nationalism at the time seemed like the only side that valued Japanese glory, especially when the alternatives either heavily promoted external support and international contribution as the solution to Japan's problems. The promises of a restrengthened military acted as the strategic framework for Japan to regain its right as one of the largest economies in the world when surrounded by threats that posed an existential risk to the state.

Lastly, the development of nationalism is oftentimes reflected in the political moves made by the government. The chaotic political landscape has given rise to a few major policy changes that are notable, the first and most significant of them being the revision of Article 9. The call for revision always existed, but only peaked as a part of the nationalist agenda. The impact of the change is not only tangible, but more importantly representative. In 2000, 41% of the Japanese public were in favour of it, but the figure rose by 6% in only twelve months, highlighting the urgency of the matter within the minds of the Japanese populace. (Wallace, 2016)

The revision, to many other countries, while suggesting a regression toward Japan's pre-war militaristic posture, was seen as glorious and necessary in the perspective of Japanese citizens as a step towards its historical pride. Furthermore, this is bolstered by the prevalence of regional geopolitical aggression in Asia, making the momentum for change in foreign policy even stronger within the state. The significance of the change is that it signaled to the world that Japan no longer wanted to abide by the world order established post-World War Two, and wanted to be freed from the reputation of a defeated power, reclaim its normal statehood and sovereign rights.

While this argument is compelling, it was an extremely bold move for Japan, as this risked large scale sanctions from its neighbours that are historically rivals of Japan. Its consequence transcends beyond diplomatic posturing and diminishing international relationships with geopolitical neighbours, but is actively depleting the practical benefit Japan can get. (Woodward, 2017) Thus, potential consequences of not collaborating with China can be enormous, specifically considering its reliance on natural resources. At this stage, Japan's desire for an army was strong enough that they are willing to give up certain capital benefits, which is sufficient to prove their intent to become a conventional state. At this moment, nationalism is perceived to hold the highest likelihood of achieving this aim, and the electoral outcomes have proven their determination for positive change.

Examining Japan's strategic partnership with the USA reveals the uniqueness of nationalism as the solution. As mentioned before, Japan had been a traditional ally of the US, and this continued, if not since strengthened, under the administrations of Koizumi and Abe, although the inadequacy of support from the US already raised public discontent. (Woodward, 2017) From the divergent diplomatic postures, it is evident that the role of Japanese nationalism here was to foster othering within the Japanese people to ensure domestic unity rather than being a genuine pursuit of self-reliance. An example of this would be Abe's idea of "Proactive Pacifism", which ultimately still links back to connections with the USA. (Atanassova-Cornelis, 2014) This claim can then be used to prove the xenophobia that is targeted at Chinese and Koreans, as well as the neglect of potential retribution from China. As long as Japan stays under the protection of the USA, all those harms can be mitigated at least. From the bold intent to revise Article 9 itself, it can be argued that the aim of the new Japanese nationalism is to unify its people towards the common enemy in Asia and encourage deeper collaboration with the USA. Furthermore, it can be inferred that the move is largely utilitarian with the extensive political rhetoric targeting the narrative of existential threats, making it more compelling in the sense that it is the necessity for the state instead of a choice.

### The Reconstruction of National Identity: War Criminal or National Hero?

The shift in diplomacy is also seen in other actions of the government, such as the revision of educational curriculum and paying official visits to Yasukuni Shrine. Both are representative of Japan's diplomatic strategy shifting from pacifism to a clear anti-Chinese stance. The revision of education, as mentioned at the start, is largely denialistic of historical events that tarnish Japan's international reputation, which necessarily includes the reevaluation of contents such as massacres and human experiments during the second Sino-Japanese war. This was extremely problematic in terms of the potential controversies and tension it brings to the nation. While pedagogical reforms target the next generation, official visits to the Yasukuni Shrine serve as a more immediate, symbolic assertion of sovereignty. The modern purpose of the shrine was to commemorate Japanese officials who died in wars, including those who are accused of being Class A war criminals in World War Two. The place is perceived as a legitimate site of veneration and is sacred in the eyes of hardline nationalists, such as Abe himself. Yet, paying official visits frequently leads to discontent from China regarding the sensitivity of the shrine. (Koichi, 2016) The explicitly shown political acts ultimately bypass the conventional limitations of law and deteriorating international perception, and redefines the cultural and spiritual significance of its history. Through honouring war criminals, the state essentially challenges the postwar pacifist order that labelled them as criminals. This radical reconstruction of societal perceptions was not only significant, but also politically unprecedented.

However, the shrine was fundamental in demonstrating the state's resolve to safeguard Japan's heritage in the eyes of the population. For instance, a 2014 Asahi Shimbun poll suggested that 60% of people in their thirties supported government officials' public visits to the shrine. (Wallace, 2016) Both Koizumi and Abe leveraged their visits as a strategic opportunity for electoral mobilization, which was a significant departure from the diplomatic caution exercised by their predecessors. Ultimately, the controversies surrounding education and Yasukuni represented a political leader's dedication for an independent Japanese identity that is not afraid of external oppression, and utilising the thereby cultivated, favorable public image and their strong association to nationalism to shift societal opinions. The subject of all three examples mentioned are highly sensitive yet representative of the Japanese identity, hinting at the subtle transformation of the concept of "Japanese identity" itself, transforming it into what nationalistic politicians intended. Overall, Japan has shown great political incentive through revisionist actions to depart from China and other neighbouring countries as a response to their "aggression". It can be inferred that the unprecedented political response was not only a radical departure from the conventional framework of diplomacy and politics, but an active reconstruction of the Japanese identity. The strategic implication of this is that nationalism will be integrated as part of the social framework in Japan in the long term, instead of a solution to only solve immediate issues Japan faces.

Throughout the contemporary political landscape of Japan, political discourse and state initiatives were largely centered around the concept of "Japanese identity", to suggest that Japanese people must form a unified national consensus to achieve the end goal of a strong and independent nation. With an overwhelmingly strong sense of fear for a perceived existential and overwhelming regional threat, it is not surprising that Japanese citizens are willing to believe in the side that is firmly pro-Japan and demonstrated their loyalty with radical steps towards the goal.

## The Declining Japanese Economy: Significance of Material Foundation of Public Discontent

The importance of economic development and stability is a primary indicator of a strong country, while this has been stagnant in Japan from the late 1990s till date. Japan has experienced a multitude of major economic contractions in the past few decades right after the Japanese economic miracle, leaving a substantial emotional gap within its population. The two most visible contributors were the 2008 global economic crisis and the "Lost Decades" of Japan's economy beginning from the 1990s until the 2010s. In both situations economic stagnation became most acute for the Japanese economy and its population. While those events were substantial in accelerating the recession, the structure that the Japanese economy operates on is already fragile and inefficient, susceptible to external shocks for various reasons such as aging and valuing seniority over merit due to the complex nature of the economic elements. (Aoki, 2013) This section explores their significance with the metric of scale, profundity and political responses, aiming to clearly differentiate the core of how separate causes ultimately catalyze the growing nationalist sentiments amongst the Japanese population. As a summary, the tangible pressure in the financial situations of Japanese households, as well as the rigid economic structure was primary in evoking and transforming the public discontent towards the support of nationalist solutions, with the impacts on them acute and tangible.

### Structural Issues in the Japanese Economy

One of the most fundamental analyses here regards the scale of economic impact on the population. This is especially prevalent in the context of Japan, a country that historically predicated its national prestige on economic prosperity. Japan as a country had suffered persistent economic stagnation through the "lost decades" (late 1990s to the early 2010s), at the same time experiencing major domestic crises and the 2008 economic crisis. Undoubtedly, the length of economic depression for an advanced country is virtually unprecedented among contemporary advanced economies. Apart from the timescale, the demographic scope of this impact is equally significant for a few reasons that lie in the economic structure of Japan. For instance, the majority of Japanese firms are protected by the government in various ways. This includes substantial amounts of subsidies for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), as well as policies such as the 1949 Foreign Exchange Law and the 1950 Foreign Investment Law that together imposed a restrictive and state-controlled structure for Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) into Japan, aimed to stabilise the economy. From this, inference can be drawn that recessions and external shocks will likely be felt by the entire economy instead of limited to certain sectors, establishing the fundament for the unconventional yet universal backlashes felt by most of the population. (Woodward, 2017)

With this said, these firms often lack the crucial competitiveness and flexibility relative to other countries due to the low competition pressure domestically which takes away their incentive to maintain a high degree of efficiency. This overall lack of positive change for most domestic corporations resulted in the vulnerability of them against external shocks such as the rising productivity of China overtaking, to say nothing of global macroeconomic shocks. Furthermore, the sector distribution of workers in Japan is also highly inefficient as a whole. Some examples include the 4% of all workers that work in agriculture as compared to the 1.4% of the US. This is complemented by the 72% manufacturing employees concentrated in small-to-medium enterprises (SMEs: firms that are less than 250 people) instead of large-scale, highly efficient corporations. These small firms relying on labor-intensive rather than capital-heavy methods in Japan are arguably much higher compared to the other developed economies such as the USA or Western nations like Germany. The inefficient allocation of human resources in Japan ultimately means that when the overall economic situation deteriorates, the primary burden falls upon the workers themselves. Furthermore, the system lacks efficient measures to reintegrate older workers into new industries, further widening the gap between Japan and other advanced countries, while leaving even more burden on the younger generation and the workforce overall. (Woodward, 2017)

The scale of dissatisfaction underscores the profound psychological impact onto the Japanese population, to the extent that they are much more willing to embrace large reforms and fiscal stimulus. Unsurprisingly, over 90% of Japan's exports consist of highly income-elastic goods, typically consumer durables such as cars and electronic appliances. At the same time, Japan as an island nation is also reliant on imported energy. It can be inferred that the stable income of the manufacturing sector in Japan is not only highly reliant on demand that is typically from Western countries, but also on the stable supply of energy. When the 2008 world economic crisis hit, both elements were taken away, and the Japanese economy experienced a severe contraction. In the first quarter of 2009, revenue from exports fell by 36.8% alone. This acutely worsened Japan's manufacturing industries, such that a prolonged period of stagnation across the entire manufacturing sector was precipitated after 2009. (Kawai and Takagi, 2007) The widespread economic precarity of the Japanese workforce during the time acutely allowed nationalist politicians to increase their appeal for a strong Japanese economy. When the 2008 Economic Crisis sufficiently proved the failure of post-war liberal order in stability, nationalists had a much stronger ground in arguing the necessity for Japan to be self-reliant. Which had directly led to the later popularity of policies such as Abenomics that proposed significant reforms.

### Evaluating the Working Demography

To start with, Japan's aging population problem is one of the most severe in the world, which indicates a contraction in the labor force and a sustained decline in aggregate economic output. The implication of this is that the dependency ratio is projected to simultaneously rise significantly, leaving more burden on future generations as a whole, while the low fertility rate remains constant. The consequence of low fertility rate is likely a decrease in output and lower wage rate due to increased cost, while less will be willing to give birth to children when their economic situation is stressful. This negative cycle of low economic growth and low fertility constitute a nationwide issue, and acts as a fundamental threat to the long-term vitality of the state.

On the ground of the working demography, systemic issues potentially exist at a greater scale. Due to the overwhelmingly high prevalence of intergenerational family businesses in Japan (Morishita, 2022) and its protectionist economic policies, traditional and conservative beliefs are institutionalised within such firms. Examples of this would be the ideal of "lifelong employment", and valuing seniority over merit when assessing workers. This structural rigidity is particularly detrimental given Japan's rapid demographic aging in the 21st century. Thus, older workers oftentimes have much less occupational mobility compared to the young generation. An inference here is that the conservative and traditional values these firms held made them the natural voting base for nationalists who put the Japanese identity at the core of their campaign. (Aoki, 2013)

The institutional response for this after the 2008 recession was a significant labor market deregulation and erosion of employment protections, leading to much less stability in the income of those that are young. This shift was characterized by the proliferation of short-term contracts for young workers while maintaining the lifetime security of senior employees. Yet, this only exacerbates the intergenerational inequality in the long term. Taking into consideration the remaining depression in manufacturing, this strategy resulted in three structural failures within the context of Japan's corporate landscape. (Aoki, 2013)

The erosion of human capital also is a prevalent issue amongst the Japanese workforce. Younger workers often endure difficulty in securing promotions, as higher positions are often kept to more senior workers. This eventually leads to difficulties in securing financial requirements and opportunities, especially when supporting a family. In addition, short-term contracts deincentivizes firms from investing in human capital, further reducing their ability to find desirable occupations afterward. Furthermore, less training of new workers leaves the firm no other option than to keep relying on senior workers with their skills that were built with experience. However, it is common for older workers to struggle to adapt their expertise to the production of high-growth modern commodities, and would instead stay with the original production that could easily be outdated. (Aoki, 2013) This mismatch between supply and demand for Japanese goods would almost certainly lead to a reduction in revenue, which exerts downward pressure on real wages. This problem is especially prevalent in the modern age due to the rise of China as the main export country with goods that are much more price competitive. The key here is that the economic stagnation is not a temporary issue, but a structural decline of the Japanese workforces' capability. This suggested that the impacts felt by the population are not only universal, but are also unlikely to be improved over time, leading to further degradation of output and economic growth. This nuance in the cause of national recession is exploited by nationalists by promising bold reforms and the return to past industrial glory.

The overall impact of the economic stagnation specifically for the younger generation can be summarized as an erosion of both real wages and the pathways to future earnings, and when both the skill level and wage decreases for younger generations, they're predisposed to seek political alternatives that promise to alleviate these systemic pressures with sufficient agency. The current workforce lives in the shadow of the Japanese economic miracle enjoyed by previous generations, and the sharp contrast in quality of life further reinforced the idea that Japan must do better, which nationalism is perceived to promote through its hardline stance on economic reforms and efforts in reducing foreign intervention.

### The Official Response

The state's policy responses, most notably the Abenomics program initiated during Shinzo Abe's second term, provide a significant insight into the electorate's socioeconomic anxieties and aspirations of the Japanese electorate. Abe's economic policies consist of a series of economic actions (Three Arrows) that utilises monetary policy, fiscal policy and structural reforms. The monetary aspect focused on the program of Quantitative and Qualitative Monetary Easing, a group of actions that aims to achieve a 2% inflation rate over two years. This program involved the massive expansion of the monetary base, executed through the Bank of Japan by purchasing an extensive amount of Japanese Government Bonds to redistribute money, as well as increasing the purchase of exchange-traded funds (ETFs) and real estate investment trusts (REITs). The fiscal side was more straightforward through the direct increase of fiscal spending to stimulate economic activities as well as infrastructure revitalization. Consumption tax was raised from 5% to 8% in 2014, and is projected to increase again to compensate for government spending. Meanwhile, the government also wanted to reduce corporation tax to encourage hiring more workers. Lastly, structural reforms with various aims were implemented. This includes support for industrial regeneration, assisting the growth of SMEs and innovative technologies, the expansion of the labor force for the female, elderly, and the young, creating new markets in various sectors ranging from healthcare to fishing, as well as globalisation. While the full scope of structural reforms faced significant bureaucratic and political hurdles, they remained central to political discourse. (Akram, 2014) Although the monetary component of the policy is most frequently mentioned in studies, the rather bold fiscal and reform components were the real stimuli that did bring short term change to the Japanese economy in 2013 following its implementation. The proposal of Abenomics very much filled in the gaps of the nationalist narrative by providing the tangible strength Japanese electorates demanded. For instance, increased spending on welfare and infrastructure represented Japan's capability to rebuild national strength and cater to its people. Furthermore, structural reforms were framed as active revitalization of the nation as another way to prove the strength of Japan. By actively creating new markets and globalisation, Abe aimed to prove to the Japanese electorate that Japan has the potential to stand against China, which accurately matched the political narrative and the psychological needs of the Japanese populace.

The implementation of these policies yields two primary insights, the first being that the importance of the issue is undoubted for the Japanese voters for Abe to use it as the core of his campaign, highlighting the relevance of those issues to people's life. The second is the emphasis on productivity, which was much less catered to previously. While the overall merit of the policy remains controversial, the assertiveness and institutional resolve displayed in the process of installing bold economic policies is evident. Inference can be made that it represented a radical departure from the incrementalism of previous administrations to the Japanese population who suffered from deflation for two decades. (Akram, 2014) The widespread support for Abenomics illustrates a clear desire from the public for a functioning economy from all demographics, but especially amongst the younger generation, who bore the disproportionate weight of structural stagnation. By framing economic policies as a collective effort of Japan, Abe was able to incorporate the nationalist ideal for a strong state into the larger, universal demand for a functioning economy.

Through the analysis of different economic aspects, it is evident that the issue of economic recession from the perspective of the Japanese populace are not simply significant but existential, especially amongst the younger generation. Concurrently, the rise of nationalism as the solution to the overwhelming problem seemed to be the most valid and tangible alternative, seen in the rapid execution of Abenomics and the large public support. The frustration of Japanese people being systematically excluded from stable employment and wage growth were eventually expressed politically through the support of a leader that they believe will revive the glory of Japan. Amongst the economic desperation, nationalism provided the necessary ideological framework for the mobilization of the Japanese population.

## From Parliament to Price: What Is It, and Why Does It Matter?

When the two major factors are considered through a unified analytical framework, it may seem that they are equally valid and strong for the development of nationalist sentiments in Japan. Although this statement is true to some extent when taking into account the complex social structure and interplay of elements, this paper contends that the economic concerns Japan faces is the primary reason for the modern form of nationalism to grow to such an extent that it now defines the contemporary political landscape. The landslide victory of Sanae Takaichi, who claimed to be the predecessor of Abe as another far-right nationalist, in 2025 with nearing 80% support rate can sufficiently prove the nationalist's political dominance. With both means already proven to be key causes and accelerators to nationalist sentiments, some criteria can be used to evaluate the relative weight of these drivers. The main aspects to consider when assessing the significance should still be the tangible immediacy and systemic profundity of their effects.

### Assessing Relative Scale of Economic Crisis and Political Disorder

On the matter of scale, where tangible impact is the primary metric for most, economic stagnation is much more relevant to the population of Japan for a few reasons. First, a defining characteristic of many advanced electorates is that a substantial portion of the population does not engage in politics. This implies that issues mentioned regarding sovereignty and external threats, may be limited to a specific subset of the population without leaving any foreseeable impact. Across the Japanese General Social Survey from 2008, 2010, and 2012, an average of 55.3% of the sample population either do not have a particular party that they support or know about. It is worth noting that these years are typically hard times for the Japanese population, with the large economic recession in between. These statistics illustrate the limited scale of active political engagement, which is the prerequisite for nationalist campaigns targeting foreign policies to be effective. (Osaka University of Commerce, 2008) (Iwai and Tanioka, 2010) (Osaka University of Commerce, 2012)

However, most people in society need to be in an occupation to live, and when both cases only consider adults, the economic variable demonstrates a significantly broader demographic reach. To make it even clearer, Japan is a country that relies heavily on both exports and imports, and the world economic crisis alongside domestic structural issues effectively curtailed the volume of Japanese exports within the relevant timeframe, which lasted until today. (Kawai and Takagi) This, alongside the large portion of workers that work in the manufacturing industry and are insufficiently protected from external shocks, provides a compelling empirical basis for the argument that the economic impacts were fundamentally impactful on the daily lives of the citizens. With this link made clear, it is more obvious that the scale of tangible impact on the economy is much more applicable and relevant compared to ones in the field of politics.

### Comparative Relevance of Factors on the Demography

External threats, although strong, are largely speculative and are based on predictions. Although this doesn't diminish the validity of the truth in it, the lack of quantifiable evidence is naturally less persuasive than more visible aspects of the same issue. There are two key perspectives to mention, the degree of impact and the demography that takes the impact. Although the potential harm of external aggression is prevalent, there are many drawbacks to the extent to which it can reach the minds of the local populace. For instance, although the incursion of China and North Korea in Japanese water represents the disregard for national sovereignty and significant danger, the impact of the event on the average citizen remains abstract unless filtered through state-led campaigns or nationalist propaganda.

The same applies to issues such as education and Yasukuni Shrine. Most important is that intense threat, or even direct danger does not inherently catalyse public mobilization; rather, without a direct link to personal welfare, such threats often result in civic inertia rather than political action. With this established, we can see that not only that the reach of political discourse is essentially restricted to a politically active minority, but also that the resonance of political shocks is mitigated as they filter down to the general populace.

On the contrary, economic turmoil is naturally relevant for the majority, if not all of the population, either directly or indirectly. The sharp reduction of export, rise of cost of production, and lack of innovation inevitably lead to the decline in economic growth, which consistently correlates with a decline in real wages. It is worth noting that economic decline can be cyclical, such that less wage leads to less spending, which then leads to reduction in aggregate demand, which leads back to less purchasing power of the workforce. Thereby, the process repeats itself. This is the key reason why governments in times of recession must encourage spending, evident in Abenomics itself.

In the debate over relevance, it is also worth looking into the demography itself, and identify the sectors of Japanese society where these impacts manifest most clearly. In the context of Japan, it is likely the young generation in their 20s and 30s. This section of the total demography is much more likely to be the primary drivers of this movement due to their proximity to the collective memory of Japan's postwar economic ascendancy, while at the same time growing up in environments that embrace traditional or patriotic values yet unexposed to the terror of war. (Wallace, 2016) This has resulted in high levels of engagement with the nationalist narratives of Japanese exceptionalism. Furthermore, lack of accurate historical education and the intentional weakening of negative impressions from the Second World war leads to a lack of acute historical consciousness. With this established, concepts such as patriotism or militarism are viewed as being aspirational and positive for the youths. This further purifies their idea of modern nationalism, and as a result is much more action-oriented as they truly believed in the glory it once brought. (Yang and Lim)

On the other hand, they are the major workforce in Japan with increasing burden due to the aging society. As previously established, the degradation of the economic landscape has led to less investment in human capital for new entrants to the workforce due to the need to cut costs, and there are very few opportunities to gain a high wage due to the valuation of seniority, resulting in a vicious cycle. This also coincides with the unwillingness of Japanese universities to invest in innovative technology and fields, resulting in an overall reduction of skills and wages for the younger generation as a whole. The point being made in this section is that the younger generation bore the brunt of the aforementioned structural failures. The preceding analysis indicates that in the context of Japan where aging continues to be a problem, the economic situation will only be much worse in the future if no effective action is taken. (Aoki, 2013)

A similarity can be seen in the generation, which is that they lived in a time when the situation rapidly changed in all aspects from prosperous to stagnation. Naturally, there is the tendency for them to gravitate toward the political movement that promises them to break the status quo. However, the distinction here is that the tangible economic burden is likely considered much more significant as a reason opposed to an idealistic appeal to national sovereignty considering the context of Japan now. The logic being that philosophically, the erosion of living standards exerts a more immediate pressure on the individual than the loss of national pride.

### Evaluation of Political Reform: Repercussion versus Reward

The last comparison to be made here is the change in the political landscape itself, which is much more contentious in the context of nationalism. When analysing changes in government actions, proposed amendments of constitutional laws, and the outspoken desire for standing army certainly is much more bold and significant compared to economic policies at first glance. This is complemented by the extensive effort to shape the collective "Japanese Identity" through the emphasis on cultural education and the glorification of the imperial past. Ultimately, the core of the new strategy adopted by nationalist politicians is the cultural significance and emotional unification instead of traditional conservative campaigns that targeted the practical aspects like infrastructure. This is not to say policies like abenomics did not support the practical side, but is to say that the policies are uniquely centered around the concept of "independent development", instead of development by itself. The differentiation here is key to their success, as it is not uncommon for policies that are similar to those proposed by the nationalists, but the "Japanese Pride" that is explicitly linked to the policies is both unique and compelling. This rhetoric resonated with the Japanese populace that has long been constrained by the postwar regime and its pacifist limitation, effectively garnering significant public support for a nationalist vision of collective resonance. (Wallace, 2016)

However, as mentioned in the economic section, some of the tangible repercussions of nationalist policies ultimately fed into the economic dissatisfaction without the population realising. The primary example of this dynamic is China, who deployed actions like economic sanctions, boycotts, and redirection of tourism from Japan to counteract Japanese actions. (Woodward, 2017) The practical impact of these political maneuvers ultimately manifested as further economic stagnation and contraction. This nuance in the outcome suggested that ultimately, the practical effect still manifested in the form of economic retaliation instead of further external pressure. This translation of political action into economic consequence suggests that although the changing political precedence regarding politics itself is arguably the strongest compared to all other aspects, the correlation between the process and the effect is inadequate to prove its overall effectiveness in persuading citizens. On the other hand, although the change in economic landscape is less revolutionary, the causal link between its economic drivers and their outcome is more direct and empirically verifiable.

In summary, both the scale and profundity of political conflicts as a mobilising force remains insufficiently resonant with the broader populace due to the nature of politics and limited tangible outcomes on those that are less engaged. On the other hand, though economic dissatisfaction amongst the population is less conspicuous, its universal generational scale ensures its primacy when compared to other aspects, not to mention the deep profundity and relevance it has to those that are impacted which leads to an overall decrease in productivity. Last, although the economic policies in the political landscape appear to be less significant compared to issues such as security, adequate justification can be provided as to why most systemic shocks across the political spectrum ultimately manifest in economic precarity. Consequently, the transition towards nationalism is driven more by the economical desire of the people rather than geopolitical threats or cultural ideologies.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, the widespread dissatisfaction caused by economic stagnancy was significantly more instrumental in the resurgence of nationalism in modern Japan as opposed to prevailing political tensions. This is primarily due to the superior relevance and tangibility of economic impacts as they manifest across Japanese society. This is seen through the comparison of major political moves and incidents that are widely recognised as representative of the development, with recurring patterns that compares ideological significance to practical importance that illustrates the political landscape in modern Japan.

While some scholars caution against overemphasizing casualties, identifying the direct correlations between underlying drivers and their political manifestations remains essential to historical analysis. Investigating these nuances and making educated synthesis acts as a mirror to reflect the beliefs of present scholars towards the past. So to answer the question: To what extent was structural economic discontent the main cause of the resurgence of right-wing Japanese nationalism from the late 1990s to the modern era as compared to geopolitical anxieties? Both the pattern across the Japanese political and social landscape and the manifest of multicausal effects has proven the weight of prosperity over politics within the modern, practical scope of the contemporary scholarly debate. Distinguishing between competing causalities is crucial in the present society due to the simple reason that this is the most accurate reflection of human nature and behaviour. By examining the causation, we are able to see the baseline of modern society, and the structure it orders upon. Historical and political research challenges the limit of human knowledge again and again by interrogating the social constructs under which we operate, only in the pursuit of a more comprehensive understanding of societal evolution.
