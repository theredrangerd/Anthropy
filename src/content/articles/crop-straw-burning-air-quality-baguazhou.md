This study investigates the extent to which the reduction of open crop straw burning, following Jiangsu's 2009 ban, has contributed to improving air quality and resident health in Baguazhou, an agricultural island in Nanjing, China, over the period of 2010 to 2025. Drawing on official PM2.5 monitoring data, published literature, and a self-conducted survey of long-term Baguazhou residents as supplementary evidence, this paper found a very strong negative correlation with PM2.5 concentrations increasingly declining from 2010 to 2025 and suggests that reduced burning correlates with improved respiratory health in Baguazhou. The paper also reveals that most farmers stopped burning primarily due to external pressure rather than environmental awareness as well as the fact that stealth burning persists, suggesting that while the straw burning ban has been substantially effective in improving air quality and associated health outcomes, the sustainability of this progress depends on strengthening education and practical infrastructure alongside enforcement.

## Introduction

To what extent does the reduction of crop straw burning contribute to improving air quality and the resulting resident health in Baguazhou, Nanjing, China, from 2010 to 2025?

Existing studies have already shown that straw burning increases particulate matter and damages health. However, these studies usually focus on wider regional or national patterns in China or at a global scale, not on a specific community such as Baguazhou, Nanjing. This study therefore adds value to the academia by linking a local policy change to local air-quality data and residents' health outcomes over a fifteen year period.

## Literature Review and Definition of Key Terms

Open biomass burning (OBB) refers to the combustion of vegetation in open areas, including forests, grasslands, shrublands, and agricultural fields. A major part of OBB comes from open crop straw burning (OCSB), which alone contributes to over 75% of total biomass burning emissions (Wen et al.), resulting in the heavy emissions of particulate matter (PM), especially PM2.5. This information is drawn from the journal Environmental Science: Atmospheres, which, according to SCImago Journal Rank (SJR) — a metric that measures journal prestige based on citation volume and the prestige of citing journals — is ranked in Q1 (Scimago), indicating that it is among the top 25% of journals in its category and is therefore a reliable source. According to the United States Environmental Protection Agency, the primary U.S. federal agency responsible for setting air quality standards, PM2.5 are fine inhalable particles with diameters that are generally 2.5 micrometers and smaller, that can get deep into human lungs and bloodstreams, causing respiratory health problems ranging from asthma attacks to cardiovascular disease and premature deaths (United States Environmental Protection Agency). Moreover, in a study published in a peer-reviewed article in Frontiers in Environmental Science, a reputable Q1 scientific journal (Scimago), OBB causes over 45% of PM2.5 concentrations around the globe (Mehmood et al.). China, as one of the world's largest agricultural producers, generates roughly 600 to 800 million tonnes of crop straw annually, which is about 20% of the total amount produced globally (Wen et al.). In China, widespread crop straw burning began in the mid-1980s as a cheap way to clear the fields after harvesting, and by the mid-1990s, it had spread across all major regions that produce grain. It was estimated that between 1997 and 2013, Chinese farmers burned about 2200 teragrams of crop stalks (Islam et al.), resulting in emissions of 1.036 million tons of PM2.5 per year, according to Proceedings of the International Academy of Ecology and Environmental Sciences, a peer-reviewed open-access journal focused on ecology and environmental sciences.

During 2003–2005, satellite remote sensing detected an average of about 12800 burning sites per year across the nation, and Jiangsu Province had one of the highest counts. With about 2248 sites annually, the density of burning sites in Jiangsu reached 219.1 sites per 10,000 square kilometers, more than 16 times the national average of 13 sites per 10,000 km² during that period, according to World Bank. As a highly reputable international institution, data from the World Bank are generally reliable for macro-level comparisons, though the specific figures on burning sites from 2003–2005 may have limitations due to satellite detection constraints and spatial aggregation.

However, this situation has changed. Starting in the late 1990s, the central government issued notices prohibiting straw burning near airports and highways, then gradually expanded the ban nationwide through stricter laws, leading to all-out bans in many provinces including Jiangsu. As a result, China achieved a sharp drop in emissions caused by crop straw burning and a rise in national straw-utilization rate, as stated in a study by Dongbei University of Finance and Economics and the University of Cambridge's Department of Land Economy, giving this piece of information high validity (Xu et al.).

## Geographical Context

Baguazhou Island, located in the northwest of Nanjing, Jiangsu Province, is an island covering 55.6 square kilometers between the Yangtze River and Jiajiang River. It is an agricultural area producing corn and rice during autumn harvest, from September to October (Baidu). Traditional straw burning created seasonal smog in Baguazhou (Xinhua News Agency), but national bans since 2009 (Standing Committee of the Eleventh Jiangsu Provincial People's Congress) promoted alternatives like composting or selling crop straw to factories that recycle crop straw, making it a suitable place to investigate how environmental regulation may affect both air quality and residents' health well-being. Although air pollution in Jiangsu has multiple sources, my study isolates crop straw burning by focusing on Baguazhou as an agricultural island where the main post-harvest residues are corn and rice straw, and where the 2009 ban directly targeted open burning of these residues.

## Hypotheses

1. After the national ban in 2009, as more years passed, there is an increased reduction of crop straw burning in Baguazhou from 2010-2025, shown by the decline in regional annual average concentration of PM2.5.
2. Between 2010-2025, reduced burning correlates with improved health among residents, indicated by reduced premature mortality caused by cardiovascular disease (CVD) and chronic respiratory diseases.

## Methodology

### Methods of Data Collection

To investigate the relationship between crop straw burning reduction and residents' well-being in Baguazhou, I used a mixed-method approach. I compiled official air quality reports from the People's Government of Nanjing that provides annual average concentration of PM2.5 every year from 2010 to 2025, published studies on Nanjing citizen health, and other research from reliable sources such as universities or institutions to examine the trend in Baguazhou's air quality after the 2009 ban and to judge whether the improvement in air quality was consistent with improvements in residents' health. These data are highly reliable because they are either city-level official monitoring results or obtained by reputable institutions. For data used as supplementing evidence, I designed a survey and distributed it to Baguazhou residents who have lived on the island for more than 30 years. I used a snowball sampling method: I first sent the questionnaire to eligible residents I knew personally, such as my relatives living in Baguazhou, and then asked them to forward it to other eligible residents they knew. This approach was chosen because eligible residents in Baguazhou generally have low levels of formal education, making it difficult to find individuals who are both willing and able to complete a questionnaire. In such a situation, snowball sampling is the most efficient way to reach the target population (Hassan). A total of 45 valid responses were collected, 15 of them from farmers and 30 from non-farmers. The survey covered topics about past and current crop straw handling methods, change in respiratory health symptoms and life satisfaction before and after the burning ban, and so on. All responses were anonymous, and I informed participants that the data would only be used for my academic research. While such self-reported survey data are useful for illustrating residents' lived experiences and for comparing with objective air quality trends, it must also be acknowledged that they are inherently subjective, vulnerable to recall bias, and based on a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability and reliability of the findings. Therefore, these data are used only as supplementary and additional evidence, rather than as the primary support for my hypotheses.

### Methods of Data Analysis

The method used is Spearman's Rank Correlation Coefficient, which measures the degree of correlation between two data sets, suitable for determining whether a significant correlation exists between years passed since 2009 and PM2.5 levels.

## Data Presentation and Analysis

### Hypothesis 1: After the National Ban in 2009, As More Years Passed, There Is an Increased Reduction of Crop Straw Burning in Baguazhou from 2010-2025, Shown by the Decline in Regional Annual Average Concentration of PM2.5

*Figure 2. Spearman's Rank Correlation Coefficient Values (Royal Geographical Society)*

*Figure 3. PM2.5 Levels (μg/m3) in Baguazhou by Years Since 2009 (南京市人民政府 [The People's Government of Nanjing])*

This line graph is constructed with official data from The People's Government of Nanjing. From the line graph, it can be observed that the PM2.5 data for Baguazhou from 2010 (year 1 after the crop straw burning ban) to 2025 (year 16) shows a clear downward trend. In 2010, the concentration was 70 μg/m3. It fluctuated in the first few years after the ban, peaking at 78 μg/m3 in 2013 and 73.8 μg/m3 in 2014, which can imply that the ban took time to be fully effective. During earlier years, it is likely that law enforcement was not sufficient and sustainable alternatives to burning crop straw had not been widely developed, so a large part of farmers still burned crop straw out of habit. However, after 2014, the PM2.5 concentrations started to drop at a steady pace. In 2025, 16 years after the ban, the annual average PM2.5 concentration reduced to as low as 27.1 μg/m3, fallen by more than 60% compared to the value in 2010. This shows that as more years passed, the effect of the ban became increasingly visible. This pattern can be explained by the fact that the 2009 Jiangsu straw-burning ban directly targeted open crop straw burning, which is a major source of PM2.5 and other particulate matter that pollutes the air, as stated in Section 1.2 (Wen et al.). Over the following years, enforcement became stricter, with more patrols, heavier fines, and clearer guidance on alternative straw disposal methods, as officially stated in reports published by The People's Government of Qixia District, Nanjing (南京栖霞区人民政府 [The People's Government of Qixia District, Nanjing]). As a result, fewer farmers continued open burning, so the reduction in burning is a plausible explanation for the decline in PM2.5 rather than a coincidence. This matches well with the observed change in farmers' behavior presented by my survey results. Before the ban, 20% of the farmers burned crop straw directly in the fields or by the road, and 60% took it home to use as fuel, much of which was likely burned as well. Only 20% of farmers chose to sell straw to recycling factories.

*Figure 3.1. Proportion of Farmers Reporting Each Crop Straw Disposal Method Before 2009 Ban*

After the ban, none of the farmers responded that they burn crop straw openly in the fields, and only one person (6.67%) still piled straw by the road to burn. The proportion of farmers burning crop straw as fuel dropped to 26.67%, and the proportion of farmers selling straw to factories rose to 46.7%, possibly because the straw recycling method is more developed and accepted. This is objectively corroborated by a Xinhua News report on Baguazhou, which describes a government-supported "enterprise plus agent" recycling system, where a local factory purchases over 40,000 tonnes of crop straw from farmers annually, farmers receive a subsidised price of 40 yuan per truckload, and one interviewed farmer earns over 50,000 yuan per year from selling straw, demonstrating that economic incentives and improved collection services have made selling crop straw a realistic and profitable alternative to burning (Xinhua News Agency). However, it must be acknowledged that survey respondents may be reluctant to admit illegal behavior even under conditions of anonymity, as open burning has been prohibited by law since 2009. Therefore, the reported post-ban burning figures likely underestimate the true extent of continued burning, which is a major limitation of the self-reported data I collected on sensitive topics. Consequently, while the survey suggests a substantial reduction in open burning, the actual persistence of straw burning may be higher than the numbers indicate.

*Figure 3.2. Scatter Graph for Spearman Correlation (Barcelona Field Studies Centre)*

*Figure 3.3. Graph of Significance Level for Spearman Correlation (Barcelona Field Studies Centre)*

When applying the Spearman's Rank Correlation Coefficient, a Spearman coefficient of –0.92 is reached, indicating a very strong negative correlation. The significance level shows that this correlation's likelihood of occurring by chance is lower than 0.1%, meaning the correlation must be accepted. This means that as the number of years after 2009 increases, PM2.5 levels in Baguazhou fall. To understand why this happened, my survey asked farmers who no longer burn straw about their main reason for stopping. 40% said it was because the government bans burning and they fear being fined. Another 40% said they stopped because they know burning pollutes the air and is bad for health. Notably, one person (6.67%) admitted that they still burn sometimes. This implies that while the ban has been effective in reducing straw burning and improving air quality, more than half of the farmers' motivation to stop came from external pressure, mainly the fear of getting caught and punished, rather than a deep internal belief that burning is harmful. Only 40% reported health and environmental awareness as their reason for stopping burning crop straw. This suggests that if enforcement weakens, many farmers are likely to return to burning. This point is further supported in an article published by Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, where Minchun Zhang, the Dean of Changsha University of Science and Technology Graduate School and Law School, stated that in the regions of China where straw burning bans are enforced, policy implementation tends to focus narrowly on achieving compliance outcomes, while public education and awareness campaigns remain insufficient. Consequently, many farmers hold resistant attitudes toward the bans (Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences). Looking at it from a farmer's perspective, it is understandable. For generations, burning was the easiest and cheapest way to clear fields after harvest (法制日报 [Legal Daily]). Changing a habit takes time, and forcing a new rule without fully changing people's understanding often leads to compliance only on the surface. This implies that when governments design environmental policies like crop straw burning bans, they often focus on enforcement, such as patrols and fines, but the importance of education and building awareness are often forgotten. If policymakers want a lasting solution, investment in teaching stakeholders the reason behind the policy is crucial. In conclusion, the reduction in crop straw burning did increasingly lower PM2.5 levels over time, and Hypothesis 1 is supported. Yet research also reveals that most farmers stopped burning because of external pressure instead of environmental awareness, which means that while air quality has improved, a stronger focus on farmers' education is needed to keep the progress fully sustainable.

### Hypothesis 2: Between 2010-2025, Reduced Burning Correlates with Improved Health Among Residents, Indicated by Reduced Premature Mortality Caused by Cardiovascular Disease (CVD) and Chronic Respiratory Diseases

Crop straw burning releases fine particulate matter into the air, and this pollution can irritate the respiratory system and damage exposed populations, as stated in Section 1.2. Therefore, when crop straw burning decreases, PM2.5 exposure also decreases, which reduces cough, throat discomfort, nasal irritation, and other respiratory symptoms. This reasoning is supported by evidence based on long-term studies published on BMC Public Health, a peer-reviewed open-access journal (Yang et al.). The journal was placed in the top 25% (Q1) of journals in its subject category by SJR ranking and has an H-Index of 225 (Scimago), showing that it is not only currently well ranked but is also frequently cited, making it a credible and prestigious source within public health. One study published on BMC Public Health investigated effects of air pollution on influenza-like illness in Nanjing, and showed that higher PM2.5 exposure leads to more cases of influenza-like respiratory illness (Huang et al.), while another study extracted data from the Death Information Registration and Management System of Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention and found that premature mortality from CVD and chronic respiratory diseases in Nanjing decreased with the average annual percent changes (AAPC) at -5.9% and -1.6% respectively, between 2007 and 2018. These findings support the view that improved air quality can contribute to improved residents' physical health. My survey results provide supplementary evidence. When asked to compare their autumn respiratory symptoms including coughing, throat discomfort and nasal discomfort between the pre-ban period (15 years ago) and now, using a 10-point scale where 1 = much worse and 10 = much better, most respondents reported that their symptoms had improved in recent years, with average scores of 7.33 and 7.38 out of 10. Although these self-reported data are less reliable than official statistics or medical studies and have the limitation that individuals may hold different personal standards for what constitutes "much better," making the scores not directly comparable across respondents and potentially introducing memory bias or varying health expectations, they are still consistent with the objective trend and show that residents themselves noticed the improvement.

This study aimed to answer: to what extent does the reduction of crop straw burning improve air quality and residents' well-being in Baguazhou from 2010 to 2025? The answer is: to a substantial extent, though limitations remain. To start with, air quality improved significantly after the 2009 ban of crop straw burning. PM2.5 levels dropped from 70 μg/m3 in 2010 to 27.1 μg/m3 in 2025. In addition, a very strong negative Spearman correlation of -0.92 confirms that this decline is closely connected to the passage of time since the ban, during which farmers gradually stopped burning straw. As a direct consequence of cleaner air, residents' respiratory health improved, indicated by published studies in Nanjing that show a reduction in premature mortality from CVD and chronic respiratory diseases over the same period. Taken together, these results make it reasonable to conclude that the reduction in straw burning was a major contributor to better air quality, which in turn led to measurable improvements in local health outcomes. However, the transition is not perfect. Yangtze Evening Post, which is Jiangsu Province's official newspaper and the evening newspaper with largest national circulation in China, reported stealth burning still exists in Baguazhou (Yangtze Evening Post). Moreover, multiple official announcements from the Qixia District government repeatedly mention the need for inspection and supervision to prevent unauthorized straw burning in Baguazhou (南京栖霞区人民政府 [The People's Government of Qixia District, Nanjing]). This shows that despite policy efforts, illegal burning persists and requires ongoing enforcement. My survey adds supplementary information: 57% of residents have seen or heard of stealth burning in Baguazhou, and the main reasons are farmers believe that burning crop straw is more convenient than bringing them to recycling companies, as well as limited accessibility of collection services. This implies that while the policy has been largely successful, leftover problems remain. The effectiveness of a ban depends not only on laws and penalties, but also on practical support and on changing farmers' habits through education.

## Conclusion

*Figure 4. Baguazhou Farmer Burning Crop Straw in the Field (Author's Personal Collection)*

According to the results, it is revealed that most farmers stopped burning not because of a deep understanding of the environmental or health risks, but because they feared fines. This offers implications for future policy design in other agricultural regions of China and beyond: compliance driven by force can initiate change, but if enforcement weakens or services offering alternatives remain inadequate, old practices are likely to return. The research therefore suggests that to achieve reduction of crop straw burning and environmental protection, having legal bans alone is insufficient, and long-term success requires investment in education to build environmental awareness, as well as in infrastructure, such as more accessible and reliable straw collection systems.

## Evaluation

### Strengths

The study has several strengths. All survey responses were anonymous, and I clearly stated that the data would be used only for academic research, which encouraged honesty. By selecting only long-term residents (over 30 years on the island), I ensured that respondents could remember the situation before the ban. The combination of primary survey data with secondary PM2.5 data allowed me to link subjective well-being to objective air quality trends.

### Limitations and Improvements

1. The sample is small (45 people) and not random – while approximately 50% of the respondents were residents I randomly selected to interview during my visit to Baguazhou, 50% were my relatives or acquaintances, which could introduce bias for that participants might not be absolutely honest when self-reporting information. A larger, randomly selected sample would give more reliable results. To overcome this in my paper, I used the survey only as additional evidence to supplement my primary evidence, which is published literature from reliable sources like renowned journals and institutions. I also explicitly acknowledged the bias and limitations of my survey data every time it was mentioned, to minimize the inaccuracies that might be caused.

2. The official PM2.5 data published by the Nanjing Government comes from a single monitoring point, so it is difficult to verify whether that point perfectly represents the whole island. Also, the data do not separate straw-burning pollution from industrial or traffic pollution. To improve this, future studies could use portable PM2.5 monitors placed near fields during harvest seasons. In the broader scheme of my paper, I addressed this by focusing on the long-term downward trend rather than specific values, and by using Spearman's rank correlation which only requires consistent relative measurements. This helps demonstrate the effect of the ban despite the lack of source-specific data.

3. Other things also changed in Baguazhou between 2010 and 2025 besides reduction of crop straw burning. New roads, cleaner cooking fuels, and other factors are all able to affect local air quality and well-being. Because my study did not control for these, I cannot claim that straw burning reduction is the only cause of the improvements, only that it is a very likely major contributor. To mitigate the influence of this limitation, I explicitly acknowledged these confounding factors in my discussion and did not claim an exclusive causation.
