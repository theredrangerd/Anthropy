This investigation explores how the nature of religious policies in the eras of both the Mughal emperor, Akbar (1556–1605), and the government of the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) (2014–), examining shifts in religious tolerance, legal frameworks, and political narratives. It argues that Akbar's model of syncretism was a pragmatic governance strategy rather than an ideological commitment to religious unity, shaped by monarchical power structures. Conversely, the BJP's policies arise from democratic imperatives, where electoral success depends on mobilizing religious identity. While Akbar's approach fostered interfaith cooperation, the BJP's policies politicized religion to consolidate power, normalizing exclusionary attitudes. This research underscores how religious policy, whether in an imperial or democratic context, serves as a tool for statecraft, shaping societal cohesion or division. By analyzing historical and contemporary governance, this study highlights the long-term implications of religious policy in shaping national identity, questioning whether pluralism remains viable in India's evolving political landscape.

## Introduction

Existing scholarship on Hindu-Muslim relations in India often examines either Akbar's religious policies or the rise of Hindutva but rarely juxtaposes them in a comparative framework. For example, Richard Eaton's *India in the Persianate Age* explores how Indo-Islamic cultural synthesis shaped governance, while Christophe Jaffrelot's *Hindu Nationalism: A Reader* traces the ideological evolution of Hindutva. However, these works do not directly connect Akbar's syncretic governance to contemporary Hindu-Muslim political dynamics under the BJP. This study bridges that gap by employing a comparative historical analysis, assessing religious inclusivity, interfaith relations in political rhetoric, historical reinterpretation, and the use of religion as a political tool.

The evaluation is based on four key criteria: (1) religious inclusivity in governance, assessed through legal policies and state-supported religious initiatives; (2) the portrayal of interfaith relations in political discourse, including rhetoric and policy narratives; (3) the role of historical interpretation in shaping national identity, particularly through education and media; and (4) the use of religion as a tool for political consolidation, comparing Akbar's integrationist approach with the BJP's majoritarian strategies. By applying these criteria, this research seeks to determine whether Akbar's legacy serves as a historical counterpoint to current sectarian politics or has been co-opted to justify exclusivist narratives in modern India. It examines how shifts in governance, legal frameworks, and public discourse reflect broader changes in the state's relationship with religious pluralism.

## Religious Tolerance in Governance

Akbar's governance institutionalized religious inclusivity, exemplified by the abolition of the jizya tax in 1564, which had long imposed a financial burden on non-Muslim subjects. Removing this tax reduced economic discrimination and symbolized state neutrality in religious matters. This shift was reinforced by Akbar's recruitment of Hindu officials, most notably Rajput leaders such as Raja Man Singh and Birbal, whose administrative influence extended beyond token representation. Their presence in key military and political positions indicated a deliberate effort to integrate Hindu elites into the Mughal state, aligning governance with the principles of Sulh-i-Kul. Historian Satish Chandra argues that these policies were not merely symbolic gestures but structural changes that redefined imperial authority as religiously pluralistic rather than exclusively Islamic.

Beyond governance, Akbar actively facilitated interfaith exchange by commissioning Persian translations of Hindu texts, including the Mahabharata and the Ramayana. These efforts made Hindu religious and philosophical traditions accessible to Persian-speaking Muslim elites, fostering intellectual engagement across faiths. Roy Burman highlights that such initiatives contributed to a shared cultural ethos, influencing later developments in both Sufism and the Bhakti movement. Akbar's reverence for Sufi saints, particularly Khwaja Muinuddin Chishti, further reinforced this synthesis. His patronage of the Ajmer Dargah, a major pilgrimage site for both Hindus and Muslims, exemplified his commitment to fostering interfaith unity. However, his religious policies were not without resistance. His Din-i-Ilahi, an eclectic spiritual doctrine combining elements of Hinduism, Islam, Christianity, and Zoroastrianism, faced skepticism from both orthodox Muslims and conservative Hindus. While Sulh-i-Kul was an inclusive administrative policy, Din-i-Ilahi reflected a more personal spiritual experiment that ultimately failed to gain widespread acceptance. Nonetheless, it underscored the extent to which Akbar sought to transcend sectarian divisions, positioning religious pluralism as a central tenet of his rule.

Richard Eaton, in *India in the Persianate Age*, contextualizes Akbar's decision to abolish the jizya tax within the broader Persianate world, arguing that Akbar's policies reflected a sophisticated blend of Persian and Indic traditions. By removing barriers to religious integration, Akbar positioned himself as an emperor above sectarian divisions, seeking to establish a political order that prioritized governance over religious orthodoxy. His support for syncretic religious movements, such as the Bhakti and Sufi traditions, further reinforced this vision. These movements, which emphasized personal devotion over rigid doctrinal distinctions, aligned with Akbar's goal of uniting his subjects under a shared cultural framework. His religious ideology, known as Din-i-Ilahi, attempted to fuse elements of different faiths into a single ethical system, demonstrating his commitment to transcending traditional religious divides.

The contrasting approaches of Akbar's religious policies and the BJP-led government's political strategies can largely be attributed to the difference between a monarchical system and a democratic framework. Akbar wielded absolute power, enabling him to implement policies aligned with his vision of unity and stability. His religious inclusivity and syncretism fostered social cohesion across his vast, diverse empire, serving as tools of governance rather than responses to public sentiment. Policies like abolishing the jizya tax and promoting Hindu institutions reflected his pragmatic approach to consolidating authority and maintaining order.

In contrast, the BJP's policies are shaped by the imperatives of democratic governance in India, where political strategies are inextricably linked to securing votes and maintaining political power through election cycles. Democracy, by its very nature, is dependent on the support of the people, and the BJP, like all political parties, must appeal to its electoral base. This has led to the emergence of a majoritarian political rhetoric, where religious identity is often employed as a means to galvanize support among the Hindu majority. In a democratic system, policies are often shaped by populist pressures, and political parties tend to cater to the sentiments of the largest voting blocks. In this context, religious nationalism, as seen in the BJP's political agenda, is not only a means of consolidating votes but also a method of mobilizing public opinion through the portrayal of religious identity as central to the nation's cultural and political future. This form of political discourse is inherently polarizing, as it seeks to define the nation in exclusionary terms, emphasizing the dominance of one religious group over others, which can alienate minorities.

Since its rise to power in 2014, the BJP has pursued legal and political measures that redefine India's secular framework along Hindutva lines. One of the most significant examples is the Citizenship Amendment Act (CAA) of 2019, which expedites Indian citizenship for non-Muslim refugees from Pakistan, Afghanistan, and Bangladesh while explicitly excluding Muslims. This law marks a departure from Akbar's integrative approach, embedding religious discrimination into the legal structure of the state. Jaffrelot argues that the CAA reconfigures Indian citizenship from a secular identity to an ethno-religious construct, aligning governance with the broader Hindutva vision.

The CAA does not merely alter citizenship laws; it redefines the very foundations of Indian identity. By legally differentiating citizenship based on religious affiliation, the state has introduced a new criterion for belonging that prioritizes historical grievance over constitutional values. Moreover, the CAA sets a precedent that could extend beyond citizenship — if religious identity becomes an accepted basis for state policy, it could influence future decisions regarding education, employment, and even voting rights.

Similarly, BJP-led state governments have introduced anti-conversion laws that criminalize religious conversions under the pretext of preventing "forced conversions." These laws, selectively enforced against Muslims and Christians, reinforce Hindu dominance in the legal framework. Priya Singh highlights how the legal codification of religious identity under the BJP not only restricts individual freedoms but also institutionalizes systemic discrimination, thereby eroding India's secular legal framework. Arshad Alam observes that pluralism is not an inherent societal condition but a political construct that must be actively maintained.

Although Akbar's policies of religious inclusion may seem fundamentally opposed to the BJP's Hindu nationalist agenda, it is important to consider them within the distinct political realities of their time. Akbar's syncretism was not an inevitable product of medieval India's sociopolitical landscape but rather a strategic response to the immediate needs of governing a vast and religiously diverse empire. The conditions that made his policies viable — absolute monarchy, centralized power, and the lack of mass political mobilization — differ fundamentally from the democratic structures shaping contemporary Indian politics.

A direct comparison of Akbar's policies and BJP-led governance reveals a fundamental shift from pluralism to exclusivism. Akbar's abolition of the jizya tax and recruitment of Hindu officials institutionalized religious inclusivity, whereas the BJP's legal framework — through the CAA and anti-conversion laws — creates institutional barriers for religious minorities. Akbar's support for Hindu texts and Sufi traditions reinforced shared cultural spaces, while the BJP's Hindutva-driven policies favor Hindu heritage at the expense of Muslim religious and cultural institutions. Most critically, Akbar's governance integrated multiple faiths into the administrative structure, while the BJP has politicized religious identity to consolidate Hindu majoritarian rule.

Under the BJP, state institutions do not merely enforce religious segregation; they actively reshape social behavior and public attitudes toward religious identity. Legal and policy shifts have institutionalized religious hierarchy, but more importantly, they have normalized exclusionary attitudes within Indian society itself, extending into education, bureaucracy, and law enforcement, and creating a feedback loop in which religious division is not just imposed by the state but also internalized by the people.

## State-Sponsored Religious Patronage

Akbar's religious patronage extended beyond mere tolerance to active engagement with multiple faiths, particularly Hinduism and Sufism. His court became a hub of intellectual and theological exchange, where scholars from diverse religious backgrounds debated matters of faith at the Ibadat Khana in Fatehpur Sikri. This inclusivity was not simply a reflection of Akbar's curiosity but a deliberate political strategy aimed at fostering unity within his vast and religiously diverse empire. Alam argues that Akbar's engagement with Islamic and Persianate political traditions was not a rejection of Islam but a strategic adaptation to India's pluralistic context. Scholars such as Lisa Balabanlilar argue, in *Imperial Identity in the Mughal Empire*, that Akbar's patronage of Hindu institutions was instrumental in cultivating loyalty among his non-Muslim subjects, reinforcing his authority through a calculated policy of cultural synthesis, including royal sponsorship of Hindu temples, participation in Hindu festivals, and even marriages with Rajput princesses.

Akbar's legal reforms were equally groundbreaking. The issuance of the Mahzar decree in 1579 marked a decisive moment in Mughal governance, positioning the emperor as the supreme arbiter of religious affairs. This decree effectively curtailed the power of the Islamic ulama, who traditionally held authority over matters of religious law, and transferred decision-making power to the emperor himself. His policies allowed Hindu legal customs to coexist with Islamic law, creating a hybrid legal system that accommodated multiple traditions without enforcing the primacy of one over the other. Burman, in *Hindu-Muslim Syncretism in India*, argues that this legal pluralism was a defining feature of Akbar's rule, setting a precedent for a governance model that prioritized social harmony over religious rigidity.

The contrast between Akbar's legal pluralism and contemporary legal developments in India is striking. Under the BJP-led government, legal frameworks have increasingly reflected a Hindu majoritarian outlook, often at the expense of India's historical traditions of religious accommodation. Jaffrelot's *Modi's India* highlights the growing politicization of religious law, particularly in matters such as citizenship policies and personal laws. The abrogation of Article 370, which granted special status to Jammu and Kashmir, and the enactment of the CAA, mark significant departures from Akbar's vision of an inclusive legal order.

The broader implications of this shift are evident in the contested nature of religious sites and legal disputes over historical narratives. Akbar's era witnessed state-sponsored support for religious institutions across faiths, exemplified by his grants to Hindu temples and his encouragement of architectural projects that blended Islamic and Hindu motifs. This legacy of shared cultural heritage is now under threat, as contemporary political discourse often seeks to emphasize religious exclusivity rather than syncretism. The Babri Masjid-Ram Janmabhoomi dispute, for instance, illustrates the growing tension between historical pluralism and modern religious nationalism. Panikkar argues that the Ayodhya agitation exemplifies how religious symbols are strategically deployed to galvanize mass political support, transforming faith-based disputes into instruments of nationalist rhetoric. The Supreme Court's 2019 verdict, which awarded the disputed land to Hindu claimants while acknowledging the illegality of the mosque's demolition, reflects the complexities of navigating historical claims within contemporary legal frameworks. Thakur critiques the selective application of secularism in India, arguing that legal rulings on religious disputes frequently accommodate majoritarian pressures, as seen in the Ayodhya verdict.

## Conclusion

This paper has explored the significant contrast between Akbar's policies of religious inclusivity and pluralism during his reign and the BJP-led government's increasingly majoritarian approach in contemporary India. Akbar's religious patronage and legal reforms reflected a pragmatic strategy aimed at fostering unity within a diverse empire, transcending sectarian divides to ensure political stability. In contrast, the BJP's approach is largely shaped by the demands of democracy, where electoral politics and religious identity often intersect to consolidate support, sometimes at the expense of national unity.

Challenges to this thesis arise from counterarguments suggesting that Akbar's policies, such as the abolition of the jizya tax and the establishment of the Ibadat Khana, were driven more by imperial pragmatism than by genuine religious tolerance. Akbar's approach to religious tolerance was revolutionary in its time, but it may have been more a tool for political stability than a genuine effort to eradicate religious prejudice. The challenge today is whether religious tolerance can be anything more than a political strategy, or whether it can foster a truly equitable society in which all faiths are genuinely embraced.

In an era where political rhetoric often exploits religious divisions, Akbar's emphasis on a shared cultural framework could offer a timely reminder. Perhaps a governance policy grounded in religious inclusivity and pluralism could once again offer a path forward for a nation struggling with internal strife. The challenge lies in translating Akbar's ideals into a modern context, where diverse voices are tolerated and embraced in the governance process. The selective manipulation of history today risks creating a fractured identity in which different groups claim ownership over past events to justify their political dominance — in contrast to Akbar's historical approach, which sought to unify his empire through a shared understanding of the past, one that acknowledged diversity rather than erasing it.
