**Research question:** To what extent do the differences in divine intervention for men and women in Greek mythology reveal Greek cultural attitudes towards women during the classical period?

## I. Introduction

In the age of artificial intelligence and space exploration, ancient Greek mythology might seem like a relic of the past. Yet these timeless tales continue to captivate our imagination and offer profound insights into human nature and societal structures. A recurring motif within these myths is the intervention of gods and goddesses in the lives of mortals. This paper examines existing scholarship on divine intervention in Greek mythology, focusing specifically on how such interventions differ for male and female mortals, and what these differences reveal about Greek cultural attitudes towards women during the Classical period — and how this goes on to suggest that the normalised mistreatment of women stemmed from the norms and attitudes showcased in the myths passed down through society.

The study of divine intervention in Greek mythology reveals a complex interplay between patriarchal power structures and the portrayal of women. Scholars have examined how myths served to legitimize and reinforce societal attitudes towards women, often depicting them with both reverence and contempt. While some myths portray marriage and motherhood as desirable conditions for women, others highlight their victimization and lack of agency. The portrayal of rape in myths, for instance, often emphasizes women's powerlessness and loss of identity without men. Research has also explored key themes such as confinement and the dowry system, which underscore the limited freedom of Greek women as reflected in mythological narratives. Some scholars have noted that even when women are portrayed as heroes in myths, their stories often culminate in downfall or death, reinforcing patriarchal norms. The role of divine intervention in these myths has been examined as a means of explaining mortal conflicts and shaping historical narratives, with gods' favors for certain individuals impacting events like the Trojan War. Overall, the study of divine intervention in Greek mythology provides insight into the complex relationship between gender, power, and societal values in ancient Greek culture.

While existing scholarship provides valuable insights into divine interventions and gender roles, several gaps remain. Further research is needed to explore the circumstances and roles of marginalized women in these myths, and the differences between intervention for male and female mortals, comparing them to their interventions with female gods and male gods respectively. In several pieces of research, only one myth is examined, or only one gender — mortal or god — is taken into account. By comparing and contrasting many myths at the same time, I believe we are able to get a more detailed and revealing answer to the extent to which Greek myths shaped societal norms. Additionally, a more nuanced understanding of the agency and motivations of male and female deities is warranted, moving beyond simplistic portrayals of myths that reinforce them as either enforcers or victims of the patriarchy.

I will attempt to answer the research question by examining the differences between the extent and expectations of divine intervention between female and male gods, and female and male mortals, to reveal similarities and differences from which we can infer how these myths influenced Greek cultural attitudes towards women during the classical period. To answer the research question, I have split my research into four distinct criteria: male gods and their interactions with male mortals, as well as with female mortals; and female gods and their interactions with female mortals, as well as with male mortals. I believe this method cross-examines all aspects and is a more thorough investigation than other studies. By comparing at least ten different myths in each section, I point out key differences in circumstances, expectations, and ideologies across Greek mythology, gaining a deeper understanding of the true meaning each myth was trying to convey and how society may have interpreted these myths during the classical period.

The significance of this question is not to be underestimated, as these myths have stood the test of time and have been passed down through generations for a reason. By understanding and comparing the different interactions between men and women, and gods and goddesses, we can infer further details about women's life in Greece and how these myths might have influenced or perpetuated societal ideology and standards. The resilience of these norms suggests that they have been a crucial part of Greek and Western culture, as these timeless themes hold symbolic depth, continuing to shape language, literature, art, and societal values across generations, serving as foundational narratives that inform both individual identity and collective heritage in the Western world.

## II. Female Gods | Female Mortals

Greek myths frequently depict goddesses exercising formidable influence, delivering severe punishments to mortals as a way to assert their dominance and highlight cultural fears surrounding female authority — as seen in the myths of Hera and Alcmene, Io, Semele, and Heracles; Athena and Arachne; Aphrodite and Psyche, Helen, and Atalanta; Demeter and Persephone; Medusa and Athena; and Artemis and Callisto. These tales were widespread, with fear and caution underlining the power of female gods, and the malice that runs through them if defied. The punishments given to female mortals in most cases go to extreme lengths that would be considered unnecessary, showcasing anxiety around female power.

To begin with the myths of Hera: Alcmene was pregnant with Heracles after Zeus disguised himself as her husband. Hera became vengeful and delayed Alcmene's labour so that Eurystheus could be born before Heracles. With Io, a priestess of Hera pursued by Zeus, Hera turned her into a cow to prevent Zeus from forming relations with her. Semele was a mortal lover of Zeus, pregnant with Dionysus; Hera tricked Semele into asking Zeus to reveal his true form, knowing that anyone who saw a god's true form would die instantly from the force of his power — and to prove his love, Zeus did so, killing her. And Hera, jealous of the infant Heracles, sent serpents to kill him as a baby, and later drove him to madness, causing him to kill his own family.

Three patterns emerge from these myths. First, the women were never entirely at fault — there was always a man, almost always Zeus, pursuing them, provoking a goddess's wrath in the form of punishment; their only fault was falling in love with (or being pursued by) a man who could never love them back. Second, rather than questioning or punishing the male god or mortal who is almost always primarily at fault, the female god turns on her own kind — female mortals — as the root cause of the problem, resulting in physical or mental disfigurement and immense pain and loss that usually should not have been theirs to bear. Third is the irony of the situation and what it translates to in real life: these myths could be summed up as Zeus cheating on Hera multiple times, yet the mortal woman is punished harshly as if it were entirely her fault. These myths, once perpetuated, could subconsciously reinforce society's standard that if a man cheats it can be forgiven easily, but if a woman does so even once, there are incredible repercussions — with the blame falling entirely on her, as she is expected to uphold her chastity and never give in to such whims until a marriage that she does not herself choose.

The second most-repeated goddess in these myths is Aphrodite. In the myth of Psyche, Psyche's beauty caused mortals to neglect Aphrodite's worship; when Psyche and Cupid fell in love, Aphrodite imposed impossible tasks on Psyche to prevent the marriage. In the story of Helen of Troy, Helen — the most beautiful woman in the world, married to Menelaus of Sparta — became the prize when Aphrodite, having asked Paris to judge who was most beautiful among herself, Athena, and Hera, orchestrated Helen and Paris falling in love as a reward for his choosing her, setting off the Trojan War. And with Atalanta, who was raised by hunters after being abandoned by her father, the terms of her eventual marriage — that whoever could outrun her would win her hand — were subverted when Aphrodite gave Hippomenes golden apples to distract her during the race, technically cheating him into marriage. The pattern reinforced again and again through these myths is that women of the classical period should only care about their beauty and their husbands, and that when faced with problems involving these two things, their instinct should be jealousy and revenge rather than logic and kindness.

A few additional myths offer further insight. In the myth of Athena and Arachne, a mortal weaver who claimed to be better than Athena, Athena challenged her to a contest; when Arachne won, an enraged Athena ripped her tapestry to shreds. Arachne, having already heard the common myths of the goddesses' wrath, hanged herself in despair — but Athena brought her back as a giant, terrifying spider, so that she and her descendants would hang in punishment forever. In the myth of Demeter and Persephone, Persephone had lived peacefully before Hades abducted her with Zeus's consent, prompting Demeter to cause a famine while searching for her daughter. Medusa, a priestess of Athena, was raped by Poseidon in Athena's own temple; angered at the desecration of her temple, Athena cursed Medusa to become a Gorgon (though some interpret this as Athena protecting Medusa from Poseidon in her own way). And Callisto, a devoted follower of Artemis, was seduced by Zeus disguised as Artemis herself, exploiting her loyalty. Throughout these myths there is again the pattern of men being in the wrong, yet women getting the blame.

Hera and Aphrodite are the goddesses most commonly associated with vengeance in myths involving female mortals. This portrayal of female power through love and marriage — their main domains — mirrors what male mortals are most afraid of in society, and is consequently portrayed in a vicious, crude manner in myths. By normalising men cheating and framing women's main goal as their own beauty and jealousy, these myths perpetuate a stereotype restricting women's access to their own choices in marriage and community — and there is also a recurring pattern of cheating in contests, where no matter what happened, the women are blamed regardless: Hippomenes cheating to win Atalanta's hand, or Arachne winning fairly yet still bearing Athena's temper.

Throughout the myths of divine intervention between female mortals and female gods, the main themes are unfair punishment and a persistent urge to control female power. Even when the women were sexually assaulted or wronged in a competitive environment by men, the women are almost always blamed. These myths normalised this behaviour in classical society, serving as cautionary tales against higher power that simply reinforced existing societal norms.

## III. Male Gods | Female Mortals

While analysing the circumstances between male gods and female mortals, interactions with mortal women often purely involve seduction, abduction, and imposition, reflecting a cultural acceptance of male dominance and the objectification of women. The contrast between male gods' fleeting, often coercive encounters with mortal women — resulting in their suffering — and the rarity of sustained, mutually beneficial relationships involving female deities and mortal men, reflects a cultural anxiety surrounding female power and sexual agency, reinforcing the trope that powerful women cannot sustain relationships with lesser men because their desires are seen as inherently destabilizing. This exposes the mentality of mortal and divine men, the vulnerability of mortal women to divine power, and society's acceptance of male entitlement over female bodies and destinies.

Greek myths expose a dark pattern: male gods relentlessly chase mortal women as trophies, using cunning or cruelty when their desires are denied. These myths can be broadly split into two categories. First, myths prominently featuring Zeus in animal disguise — Zeus and Io, Danaë, Leda and the Swan, Europa, and Semele. Second, instances of rejection followed by retribution — Cassandra and Apollo, Hades and Persephone, Daphne and Apollo, and Poseidon and Amphitrite. Most of these stories were not treated as scandalous at the time simply because they were broadly accepted acts by classical society's standards; we must now examine them precisely because that mistreatment of women is no longer socially accepted. These myths reveal how male gods viewed women as a prize to win rather than a mortal soul.

Beginning with the myths of animalistic pursuit: Zeus is the primary perpetrator, turning himself into a cow-hiding disguise to conceal Io from Hera and forcing her to wander aimlessly for years, despite supposedly loving her. He turns into a swan to force himself on Leda, Queen of Sparta, who later bore Clytemnestra and Helen of Troy. He transforms into a bull to abduct Europa, a princess he admired. He impregnates Semele, only for Hera's trickery to force him to reveal his true godly form to her — killing her instantly with its blinding light. Danaë was imprisoned by her father to prevent a prophecy, yet Zeus reaches her in the form of a golden shower. In none of these cases is Zeus ever described as falling for the woman's kindness or personality — the gods pursue them almost always for their outward beauty, then discard them, sometimes with children, as if the women were nothing but playthings. This pattern of carelessness and arrogance — donning a persona and moving from one mortal woman to the next without proper care — reflects a broader lack of respect for mortal women, occasionally excepting a few exceptional women (such as Psyche, Atalanta, and Clytemnestra) who nonetheless ended up married to a man regardless. As this portrayal recurs across myths, the idea that women exist to serve male desire became normalized in classical Greek society.

In the cases where mortal women faced a male god and refused his advances, disastrous events would follow. In the myth of Hades and Persephone, the god of the Underworld abducted Persephone to make her his queen — notably, only with Zeus's permission, showing that to the male gods, abducting an innocent woman for their own desire was normal. Medusa, a virgin priestess in the temple of Athena, was raped by Poseidon within the temple precisely because she refused his advances, and was then cursed for her resulting anger. In the myth of Daphne and Apollo, the god physically chased her until she had to appeal to Zeus to save her — Zeus, out of pity, turned her into a laurel tree to preserve her chastity, the very thing Apollo pursued her for. Poseidon pursued the sea nymph Amphitrite to be his wife, and after repeated rejection, coerced her into marriage. And in the myth of Apollo and Cassandra, Apollo bestowed the gift of prophecy in an attempt to seduce her; when she still refused him, he cursed her so that no one would ever believe her prophecies again — a curse whose consequences arguably extended to the fall of Troy itself, since nobody heeded her warnings about the Trojan Horse. A recurring pattern is that women only fled in instances where their chastity had to be protected — a mixture of fear of the god and the societal pressure to preserve their virginity — even as the very gods enforcing those standards were the ones violating them. In the end, the women were never afforded genuine freedom of choice, mirroring how men and kings in classical Greek life took multiple wives and rarely treated women as equals.

It is notable that Zeus, the god associated with marriage, is responsible for the majority of these infidelities involving male gods and mortal women — a significant power imbalance in which male gods act with little accountability while female mortals bear the consequences. Tales of male gods and mortal women reveal a consistent pattern of male impunity and female punishment, epitomized by Zeus's own infidelities, underscoring a profound power imbalance that reinforced patriarchal norms within classical society, where male dominance was normalized and female agency was suppressed.

## IV. Female Gods | Male Mortals

Turning to the expectations that female gods held towards mortal men, their interactions typically involved guidance, assistance, and the reinforcement of heroic ideals. While the gods bestowed gifts upon mortals, they expected male heroes to wield those gifts with power and glory, most often to complete quests, achieve greatness, or win over a woman — whereas when female mortals were given gifts, it was often under the guise of punishment for the perceived misuse of divine favor, or for exceeding prescribed boundaries. These stories portray women as supportive figures who contribute to male success and the maintenance of social order, reinforcing traditional gender roles in which women's primary role is to empower men. The patterns offered by the female gods here are far more mundane and heartfelt than those of the male gods towards female mortals.

Interactions between female gods and male mortals were often pure-hearted, primarily involving guiding and gifting, and — in the rarer cases involving love — with consent from both parties. Myths of gifts and guidance include Athena and Odysseus, Hera and Jason, Athena and Perseus, Athena and Heracles, and Athena and Achilles, all portraying female gods as bringers of wisdom. Myths where emotions run higher include Aphrodite and Adonis, Artemis and Hippolytus, Aphrodite and Paris, and Artemis and Orion.

Athena actively guides Odysseus on his way home, representing wisdom and strategic power. Hera assists Jason in his quest for the Golden Fleece, reflecting her selective support for male heroes aligned with her interests. Athena provides Perseus with the tools to defeat Medusa, reinforcing her role as patroness of male heroes; she aids Heracles through his labours, and intervenes in Achilles' fate in the *Iliad* by offering guidance. Her repeated pattern of offering guidance and wisdom emphasises that even the mightiest men depend on divine women for success in battle. Hera offers Paris power, leading to the Trojan War — though this particular gift is given out of ill intention, born from revenge over a contest, indicating how female gods can sometimes manipulate men's actions to serve their own agendas, often leading to chaos and destruction. An important point is that no matter the goddess's own intentions, she never turns her power against the mortal she is aiding — at most, unjustly given power adjusts the fate of the event as a whole rather than the fate of the mortal themselves. This stands in stark contrast to how male gods treat female mortals, and suggests a balance of power, friendship, and mutual respect between female gods and male mortals. This could stem from either the male mortal believing himself to be of more equal value to a female god than he would ever be to a male god, or from female gods being wiser and more emotionally intelligent than the male gods, better understanding the respect mortals deserve. From this we can infer that society expected women to be kind and considerate to whomever they spoke to, regardless of the treatment they received in return, while men could speak to women however they wished.

Another common form of interaction was allegiance or love. Aphrodite fell in love with the mortal hunter Adonis, and when he was killed by a boar while hunting, her grief led her to create the annual cycle of rebirth symbolized by spring flowers. Hippolytus, a devotee of Artemis, rejected love and was falsely accused of rape, cursed to death by his own father Theseus — Artemis honoured him by revealing the truth the next day, showcasing her loyalty. Aphrodite saves Paris during his duel with Menelaus, emphasizing her role in protecting those she favours despite their flaws. And Artemis, while hunting with her companion Orion, accidentally shoots him with an arrow — one of the rare myths where a powerful male figure's fate is shaped by a woman's intervention. Notably, the romantic interactions between female gods and male mortals tend to end in death; even where genuine love existed, the story remains tainted by sorrow, suggesting that the moment powerful women love, it often ends tragically — while myths like Athena's continuous guidance simply reinforce the traditional role of women as those who empower men.

That the husband of the goddess of marriage is responsible for the majority of infidelities among male gods warrants attention in itself — a power play not only between gods and mortals but within the gods themselves. Even the most powerful goddesses, such as Athena, the goddess of warfare, must ask Zeus's permission before intervening in matters like war. These myths act as a mirror: even when women have been profoundly wronged, they are discarded and set aside — a reflection of how little regard ancient Greek society held for women's well-being.

## V. Male Gods | Male Mortals

Finally, to fully deepen our understanding of these myths, we must examine the expectations and circumstances between male gods and male mortals, which emphasize mentorship, legacy, and the validation of ego through acts of heroism. The contrast between the glorified, often heroic circumstances of mortal men and the horrific, desperate circumstances of mortal women in divine intervention reflects a cultural devaluation of female well-being alongside a prioritisation of male achievement. A pattern of paternal support and patronage emerges: mortal men are often part of, or venturing on, a quest in which male gods aid them. In both male- and female-god intervention regarding male mortals, the primary circumstance is guidance and gifting — a stark contrast to intervention with female mortals, which differs drastically, often stripping away their basic rights. These narratives underscore the importance of male lineage, the pursuit of glory, and the role of divine approval in achieving lasting fame, revealing a culture built on perpetuating male dominance.

The primary type of story between male gods and male mortals involves a god offering a gift or imparting wisdom, with a second, less common type involving romantic relationships, mostly with Apollo or Dionysus. The stories of Zeus and Hector, Apollo and Orpheus, Hermes and Odysseus, Poseidon and Theseus, Zeus and Perseus, and Zeus and Achilles all showcase the gods' passion for carrying out their legacy and helping mortal men, with few stipulations. There are romantic myths, such as Apollo and Hyacinthus, and paternal myths, such as Apollo and Asclepius — and an opposite case, Apollo and Phaethon. These tales showcase the glorification of the trials men go through, in stark contrast to the little recognition given to female mortals.

Zeus protects Hector during battles in the *Iliad*, and intervenes to ensure Achilles' fate is fulfilled. He protects Perseus by providing him with divine tools for his quest. Apollo gifts Orpheus a lyre, showcasing artistic patronage, and extends Admetus's life by tricking the fates, showing favour to virtuous men. Poseidon aids his son Theseus during his adventures. Hermes gives Odysseus a herb to resist Circe's magic, divine protection rewarding cleverness. These myths are short, sweet, and to the point — the gods simply aiding heroes to protect their legacy and lineage. Notably, myths from this era never depicted women as heroes in this way; that was simply unheard of. Apollo and Hyacinthus is a well-known myth of male gods loving deeply — Apollo gave up his shrine and his music to spend time with Hyacinthus, and when Hyacinthus died playing discus, Apollo, heartbroken, turned him into a flower. Apollo also helps his son Asclepius become a great healer, showcasing male familial bonds and participation in the success of male offspring, reinforcing the importance of legacy and bloodlines. Sometimes the gods took this too far — Apollo gives his son Phaethon control of the sun chariot, leading to disaster, a lesson in divine punishment that nonetheless shows male mortals exercising a degree of autonomy that female mortals were rarely granted.

The stories of male gods bestowing power and greatness upon male mortals mirror how society functioned — and still functions today. Men and their legacy were paramount, in stark contrast to the horrors so often visited on mortal women in these myths. The pattern revealed is that men in power during the classical period viewed women as objects to be handled with little respect, exercising near-total control over women's bodies and rights.

## VI. Conclusion

In conclusion, the differences in divine intervention between female and male gods, and between female and male mortals, reveal important insights into Greek cultural attitudes towards women during the classical period. Female mortals often faced harsh and unfair punishments, while male gods frequently acted with little regard for women's agency. Female gods, in contrast, tended to support male mortals, reinforcing traditional gender roles in which women's power was limited to nurturing and guidance. To further deepen the power imbalance between mortal men and women, male gods gave little respect to women while endlessly bestowing gifts upon men. These patterns highlight societal anxieties about female power and the normalization of male dominance.

While some may argue these myths reflect broader themes of divine order or cautionary lessons, the consistent blaming and suffering of women cannot be overlooked. These stories reveal how ancient Greek society justified unequal treatment of women and reinforced restrictive gender norms. So, to answer the research question: the patterns across all four combinations of mortal and divine gender show that the attitudes and expectations regarding women were significantly lower and more derogatory in these myths, which served as guidelines for regular society during the classical period.

Understanding the significance of these myths matters because they shaped foundational cultural values for the Western world. By examining these ancient narratives, we see how far society has progressed in gender equality and inclusivity as a whole, and why it remains crucial to challenge the lingering stereotypes rooted in these early stories. These stories show us where we have been — and inspire where we must go.
