This paper examines how the weakening of Pax Americana through U.S. reciprocal tariffs (2025–2026) has affected economic interdependence within the European Union (EU). Using a qualitative, document-based methodology, it analyses policy reports, economic studies, institutional publications, and trade data from sources including the European Parliament, European Commission, IMF, Reuters, and Bruegel. The findings show that U.S. tariffs disrupted transatlantic trade by reducing EU exports, increasing investment uncertainty, and weakening confidence in the American-led economic order. In response, the EU accelerated efforts to strengthen strategic autonomy, improve supply-chain resilience, deepen defence cooperation, and enhance capital-market integration. However, deeper collaboration remains constrained by differing national interests, uneven exposure to tariffs, and complex decision-making processes. While full economic detachment from the United States remains unlikely, the tariff shock has encouraged greater European coordination and self-reliance. Overall, the study concludes that the decline of Pax Americana has strengthened EU economic interdependence, though integration remains limited by political and institutional challenges.

## Research Question

To what extent does the fall of pax America due to reciprocal tariffs from the United States (2025-2026) affect the economic interdependence of the European Union?

## Introduction

The idea of Pax Americana refers to the post-1945 international order in which the United States played the central role in securing global stability through trade leadership, military protection, and the spread of liberal economic rules such as reducing trade tariffs and establishing the USD to be the world's primary reserve currency. During this time period, US hegemony catalysed international stability through the formation of multi-lateral institutions like the IMF and the UN as well as security alliances (NATO) that was contingent on the American dominance in military power (International Monetary Fund, Oct. 2025). That order did not mean the absence of conflict, but it did mean that many allies, including European states, could rely on American power to sustain predictable trade and security conditions. In 2025 and 2026, however, reciprocal tariffs and a more volatile U.S. trade posture weakened the transatlantic environment, forcing the European Union to reassess the extent to which its prosperity could continue to depend on America's willingness to uphold open markets. This development emerged from a broader shift in U.S. trade policy, in which tariffs were increasingly deployed as instruments of economic pressure rather than as exceptions within a rules-based order. As a result, European policymakers began to question the reliability of the United States as the central guarantor of global trade stability. The EU's response has therefore moved beyond short-term concern over exports and toward deeper questions of strategic autonomy, supply-chain resilience, and the long-term security of European economic interdependence. (European Parliament Research Service, 2026).

This paper will explore the deconstruction of Pax Americana due to reciprocal tariffs from the US and whether or not it has accelerated economic cooperation inside the EU, especially through strategic autonomy, supply-chain resilience, and joint investment.

## Methodology

This paper adopts a qualitative, document-based methodology to examine how the weakening of Pax Americana through U.S. reciprocal tariffs in 2025–2026 affects the European Union's economic interdependence.

The methodology is grounded in policy analysis and comparative interpretation of secondary sources. It draws on European Parliament briefings and studies, European Commission Security Action for Europe material, IMF regional economic reporting, Bruegel and Center of Economic Policy Research analysis, Intereconomics commentary, and websites such as Reuters and ING reporting on tariff effects. These sources are used because they combine institutional evidence, macroeconomic context, and current trade data, allowing the paper to connect short-term tariff disruption with broader structural changes in the EU economy.

A major gap in the current literature is that U.S. tariffs are often discussed mainly as a transatlantic trade issue or as a temporary export shock. Less attention is given to how tariff volatility changes the internal dynamics of the EU itself. This paper addresses that gap by showing that external trade pressure can increase intra-EU interdependence, making cooperation on investment, resilience, defence, and supply chains more necessary. In this sense, the study shifts the focus from the tariff shock alone to the way European states respond collectively to it.

The data used in this paper are secondary and mostly qualitative, supplemented by selected quantitative indicators. These include export declines, trade-surplus changes, and growth forecasts that illustrate the economic consequences of tariff instability. The evidence also covers policy developments in defence funding, energy security, and capital-market integration, which help demonstrate how the EU is adapting institutionally. Together, these materials support a broader political-economic reading of the tariff shock.

This method is appropriate because the research question is structural and interpretive rather than purely numerical. A qualitative case-study approach makes it possible to trace how external pressure influences European decision-making across several policy areas at once. It is also suited to a recent and still developing issue, where institutional responses and economic effects are evolving too quickly for a narrowly statistical approach to capture the full picture.

## Literature Review

The existing literature on multipolarity, strategic autonomy, and the changing international order provides important theoretical foundations for understanding how the weakening of Pax Americana may influence economic interdependence within the European Union (EU). While scholars generally agree that the international system is becoming more multipolar, they differ on the extent to which the EU can emerge as an autonomous geopolitical actor and on the mechanisms through which it can respond to declining American leadership.

Bernd von Muenchow-Pohl argues that the twenty-first century is characterised by a redistribution of power away from the traditional Western core and towards a more multipolar international order. He suggests that globalization, shifting economic power, and the growing importance of non-traditional security challenges require actors such as the EU to redefine their role in global governance. The author questions whether the EU can become a genuine strategic actor capable of projecting influence beyond its immediate neighbourhood and contributing to the management of global order. This perspective is valuable because it frames the EU not simply as an economic bloc but as a potential geopolitical power operating in a post-American international system.

Similarly, Okano-Heijmans and Sundar argue that growing uncertainty generated by Brexit, U.S. trade protectionism, and China's expanding influence has increased the importance of strategic economic cooperation. They contend that geo-economics is becoming a dominant feature of international relations and that states must increasingly integrate economic and political objectives. Their analysis emphasizes the importance of sustainable connectivity, institutional cooperation, and coordinated economic frameworks as tools for maintaining prosperity and stability in an increasingly competitive international environment. The authors further argue that cooperation among like-minded partners is necessary to preserve a rules-based international order and respond to challenges posed by changing global power dynamics.

More recent literature focuses specifically on the impact of Donald Trump's return to office and its implications for Europe. Simonet argues that the second Trump administration has exposed long-standing structural weaknesses within the EU while simultaneously accelerating efforts toward strategic autonomy. According to Simonet, American hostility towards European institutions, uncertainty regarding security guarantees, and increasing economic tensions have forced European policymakers to reconsider their dependence on the United States. The author suggests that the changing transatlantic relationship acts as a catalyst for greater European cooperation, particularly in defence, industrial policy, and economic governance. However, he also notes that internal divisions between member states remain a significant obstacle to deeper integration.

Although these studies provide valuable insights into multipolarity, strategic autonomy, and the evolving transatlantic relationship, relatively little research has examined how recent U.S. reciprocal tariffs specifically affect economic interdependence within the EU. Much of the literature focuses on geopolitical competition, security cooperation, or the EU's external partnerships rather than the internal economic consequences of declining American leadership. This research seeks to address that gap by investigating whether the weakening of Pax Americana through U.S. tariff policies has encouraged deeper economic coordination and integration among EU member states. By focusing on the relationship between external economic pressure and internal European cooperation, this study contributes to ongoing debates about the future of European integration in an increasingly multipolar world.

## Background Information on the EU

The EU's creation was shaped by a desire to prevent another destructive cycle of war in Europe. At its core, the Union was created to promote peace, stability, solidarity, and shared prosperity through economic integration rather than rivalry. The founding logic was that if states became more economically interdependent, conflict would become less attractive and cooperation more rewarding. Over time, those ideals expanded into a broader political project built around democracy, the rule of law, equality between member states, and the protection of common European values which looks like democracy and human dignity to freedom. This is why the EU is not just an economic based partnership but also a rock solid alliance which tries to bind countries together through shared long-term commitments and goals.

The European Union is a highly institutionalised political and economic union made up of 27 member states, established on November 1, 1993. The current structure of the EU is made up of five key institutions: the European Commission, which has the right to propose legislation; the Council of the European Union, where national ministers negotiate and adopt laws; the European Parliament, which holds authority over legislation and budget allocation; the European Council, which dictates the political agenda and direction of the EU; and the Court of Justice of the European Union, which helps ensure EU law is applied consistently across all member states. By establishing checks and balances between the EU's competences, this methodical division ensures that there isn't a consolidation of power that allows one party to be the unipolar force in finalising all decisions.

One of the EU's most important structural features is the single market. The single market is designed to guarantee the free movement of goods, services, capital, and persons across member states, which reduces internal barriers such as developmental gaps between nations and unequal distribution of economic benefit where less developed member states struggle to compete with wealthier partners. This in term encourages deeper economic integration by allowing all countries to expand their market size, ensure supply chain resilience, and increase foreign direct investment. This is one of the main reasons the EU is able to produce strong economic interdependence: firms can source inputs across borders, investors can move capital more easily, and workers can live and work in different member states. However, the same openness also means that economic shocks spread quickly from one country to another, making coordination necessary but also more urgent. The need for coordination is not unprecedented. During the COVID-19 pandemic, disruptions to European supply chains and shortages of critical goods quickly spread across member states, leading the EU to coordinate vaccine procurement and launch the NextGenerationEU recovery fund. This experience reinforced the importance of collective responses to cross-border economic shocks and helps explain why recent U.S. tariff measures have renewed calls for greater European strategic autonomy and internal cooperation.

The European Union is also characterised by significant economic and political differences between member states. Variations in economic size, industrial capacity, geopolitical priorities, and exposure to international trade mean that member states often enter negotiations with different interests and levels of vulnerability. These differences can complicate cooperation and make collective decision-making more challenging.

The institutional balance inside the EU creates both strength and friction. The Commission has the right of initiative, which means it is usually the institution that begins the legislative process, but it cannot impose policy alone. For most laws and trade-related measures, the Commission must work through the Council and the European Parliament, both of which can amend, delay, or reject proposals. In trade policy, the Council often uses qualified majority voting, which should in theory make decisions easier than unanimity, but in practice governments often seek consensus because trade deals are politically sensitive and affect domestic industries differently. This is especially true when agreements involve services, investment protection, or other mixed-competence questions that touch on both EU and national authority. As a result, even though the EU has the tools to act collectively, its decision-making process often requires prolonged negotiation among actors with different priorities, which slows down economic cooperation.

The institutional setup also creates a deeper problem because member states display differing perspectives and interests. Larger states such as Germany and France often have more influence because of their economic scale, diplomatic weight, and ability to shape debate through their central role in the Union. Smaller states may have less structural influence, but they can still become important in negotiations because EU rules give them formal equality in key institutions and because coalitions of smaller countries can sometimes block or reshape proposals. This looks like the "One State, One Commissioner" rule that ensures every country, regardless of its size, has a formally equal seat and portfolio representation on the EU's executive body. This makes the EU powerful because it can aggregate the interests of many states into a single market and a common external trade policy. However, this equally makes the EU fragile because those same states often disagree about how much authority should be centralized, how costs should be shared, and how far integration should go. This is essential towards understanding how the decline of Pax Americans may push the EU toward greater coordination and interdependence. The Union's own structure ensures that cooperation will always remain a matter of compromise rather than automatic unity but is subject to change when the interests of European countries are forced to align.

## The Implication of US Reciprocal Tariffs

The return of reciprocal tariffs on February 20, 2026 from the United States changes both the economic infrastructure and the political logic behind global cooperation. In the short term, tariffs raise costs for exporters, disrupt supply chains, and make firms more cautious about investing. In the long term, they weaken trust in the American-led world order built upon stability and give the EU more opportunity and reason to build self-reliance through deeper internal cooperation, stronger industrial policy, and more resilient supply networks.

For decades, the American-led order rested on the idea that the U.S. would support open markets, predictable trade rules, and broad economic stability. Reciprocal tariffs challenge that system because they show that trade deficits can be used as pressure to counteract trade surpluses of other countries and boost domestic industry competitiveness. This protectionist measure serves as a warning sign for countries all around the world, encouraging countries to begin to diversify their investment in America. In the case of the EU, member states participate more in regional cooperation, domestic production, and alternative trade links. In other words, tariffs do not just raise prices, they also change expectations of country partnerships. Once businesses and governments expect future tariff shocks, they stop planning on the assumption that global trade will stay open and stable.

For the EU, the most obvious effect of tariffs is on exports. Reuters reported in April 2026 that EU exports to the United States fell by 26.4% in February, while the EU's trade surplus with the rest of the world shrank by 60% (Reuters, 17 Apr. 2026). That is a large decline, and it shows that tariffs are not a small policy adjustment but a real shock to European trade performance. The European Parliament also noted that higher U.S. tariffs and ongoing trade uncertainty have weighed on EU economic activity, with some countries and sectors facing much larger risks than others. This is due to the fact that some member states are more export driven and sector reliant than others. For example, smaller and more trade-dependent economies such as Ireland, Belgium, and Luxembourg are particularly exposed because they rely heavily on foreign investment, external trade, and the stability of the single market. As a result, they have less capacity to absorb tariff-related shocks independently and are often more dependent on coordinated EU responses. This uneven impact signifies how the tariff shock does not hit "Europe" in a single way. Some member states lose more sales, others face higher input costs, and some are affected mainly through supply-chain links rather than direct exports (European Parliament Research Service, 2026).

Tariffs also affect supply chains in a more hidden but equally serious way. Modern European production depends on long chains of suppliers across borders. A German manufacturer may rely on parts from the Netherlands, chemicals from Belgium, and logistics through ports in Spain or France. If tariffs make transatlantic trade more unstable, firms start to rethink where they source materials, where they sell products, and whether they should move production closer to final markets. This matters because tariff-driven uncertainty does not only reduce trade volumes; it also reshapes where Europe invests, produces, and competes, thereby affecting the EU's long-term economic cohesion and strategic autonomy. An article from think.ing also warned that the U.S. tariff shock is likely to reduce EU exports by more than 4% in both 2025 and 2026, and that the new tariff environment makes Europe less competitive in the U.S. market (Ruben Dewitte, 2026). Over time, that can push firms to reroute supply chains, shift investment, or avoid making new commitments in Europe if they fear weaker access to the U.S. market.

Investment is another major channel of impact. When tariffs are unpredictable, companies delay long-term decisions. They may hold off on building new factories, expanding logistics networks, or entering new partnerships because they cannot tell what the trade environment will look like next year. The European Parliament report says trade-policy uncertainty and tariff developments are expected to dampen euro area growth by around 0.5 percentage points in 2026–2027 (European Parliament Research Service, 2026). That figure matters because it shows tariffs are not just a temporary business problem. Rather, they can reduce growth, confidence, and productivity across the whole euro area. This creates a trickle-down effect: firms invest less due to anticipated declines in profits and rising costs, resulting in slower business expansion, fewer job opportunities, and weaker innovation. Over time, lower investment reduces the economy's productive capacity, making it more difficult for the euro area to sustain growth, remain internationally competitive, and recover from future economic shocks.

These short-term effects feed into a longer-term political shift. The more the U.S. uses tariffs, the more Europe sees the need to protect itself through strategic autonomy and self-reliance in the sense of producing strategic technology like missile defense systems themselves. The European Parliament's study on strategic autonomy says that economic security has become a top priority because of supply chain shocks caused by tariffs and that Europe must build resilience in impactful areas such as defense production for both competitiveness and security, energy supply, semiconductors, and critical raw materials (European Parliament Research Service, 2026). Currently, the EU is extremely dependent on the United States for these crucial areas because of their difference in industrial capabilities and their historical relationship with the country. This does not mean Europe wants to cut itself off from the U.S. The logic is simple: if Europe depends too much on outside powers, then every tariff, sanction, or political dispute becomes a threat to the supply chain security. A good example is the EU's Security Action for Europe defence instrument. The Commission has used SAFE to support joint defence procurement and to help member states buy equipment together instead of separately (European Commission, 26 Jan. 2026). This matters because cooperation reduces fragmentation between countries over whether or not joint defence between member states should occur. If countries each buy their own systems, Europe stays dependent on outside suppliers and misses opportunities to build scale. If they buy together, they strengthen the European industry and make their defence base more efficient. That is a concrete example of how external pressure, including tariff pressure and wider geopolitical uncertainty, can push EU countries to cooperate more closely. Another example is the EU's push to deepen the single market and improve capital markets. Reuters reported that the European Commission is attempting to remove long-standing barriers to cross-border investment and deepen EU capital markets because Europe continues to export a significant share of its savings abroad, particularly to the United States. Despite possessing high levels of household and institutional savings, European capital markets remain fragmented along national lines, with different regulatory frameworks, taxation systems, insolvency rules, and financial practices discouraging investment across member states. As a result, investors often find it easier and more profitable to place capital in the larger and more integrated U.S. financial market. This has led to a situation in which European savings help finance American innovation, technology firms, and economic growth rather than supporting investment within the EU itself.

The destabilisation of the transatlantic environment through reciprocal tariffs and growing uncertainty over U.S. economic leadership has made this dependence increasingly problematic. If access to the American market becomes less predictable, Europe risks becoming vulnerable not only through trade but also through its reliance on external financial destinations for investment returns. Consequently, the Commission's efforts to strengthen the Capital Markets Union are not merely economic reforms; they represent an attempt to retain European capital within Europe, improve access to finance for European firms, and reduce strategic dependence on an increasingly volatile external partner. From this perspective, the EU is not simply reacting defensively to tariffs but is actively seeking to build stronger internal economic structures capable of supporting long-term resilience and strategic autonomy (Reuters, 17 Apr. 2026).

In the long term, tariffs may actually speed up a reorganization of the global economy so that modern day economies can hedge against geopolitical risk and US protectionism. Instead of one dominant American-led system, the world may move toward a more fragmented order made up of regional blocs and strategic partnerships. Ignacio García Bercero, a senior fellow at the institute of Brugel who specializes in trade policy, argues that Europe should respond by strengthening the single market, investing more across borders, and building coalitions with other economies that share its rules-based outlook, focused on reduced dependency on one singular outside power. Europe wants to remain connected to the world, but on terms that make it less vulnerable to U.S. political swings (Centre for Economic Policy Research).

The effect on collaboration within the EU is therefore very important. Tariffs create pressure that can bring member states closer together because they show how much they gain from acting as a group. At the same time, the response is not easy because member states do not all want the same thing. Export-heavy countries may want open markets and fast deals, while others may prefer stronger protection, more subsidies, or more national control. Some states want common debt and joint procurement; others worry about sovereignty and budget limits. This means the EU's cooperation is likely to become deeper. Countries may work together more in areas where joint action clearly improves security or competitiveness, but internal disagreement will still limit how far integration can go.

## Limits of Collaboration

### Practicality: Can Europe Detach from the American-Led Economy?

It is not realistic for the EU to fully detach from the American-led economy in the short term. The U.S. remains one of the EU's largest export markets, and the Eurostat and European Parliament data show that the American market is central to Europe's trade balance. In 2024, the U.S. accounted for about 21% of EU exports by destination, which makes it the EU's single largest export market outside the block itself (ING, Ruben Dewitte, 2026). That means any sharp break with the U.S. would immediately hurt major European industries, especially cars, pharmaceuticals, machinery, chemicals, and industrial goods. A better way to describe Europe's position is not "detachment," but "partial de-risking." Europe can reduce its dependence in some areas, but it cannot quickly replace the U.S. market with another market of the same size and wealth.

That is why the tariff shock matters. According to Ruben, EU exports to the U.S. fell by more than 4% in both 2025 and 2026, and U.S. tariffs reduced Europe's competitiveness in the American market. Reuters also reported a 26.4% drop in EU exports to the U.S. in February 2026, showing that the pressure is already visible in trade flows (ING, Ruben Dewitte, 2026). These figures show that Europe is not in a position to "walk away" from the U.S. economy without real damage. Instead, the EU is being pushed to find ways to self-sustain itself while still staying linked to the U.S. market.

### Timeframe: Why Agreements Are Long Term

This slow pace matters in a tariff crisis. Tariffs move fast, but EU law moves slowly. If the U.S. changes trade rules quickly, Europe has to respond through a system built for compromise rather than speed. That can make the EU look weak, but it also protects member states from being overruled too easily. The problem is that slow decision-making can be costly when businesses need clarity now. Firms need to know whether tariffs will last, whether subsidies will change, and whether new trade routes are being built. So while EU cooperation is possible, the timeframe often makes it less effective than a single national response would be.

### Unity: Disagreement Between Member States

A major obstacle to economic collaboration is that the member states do not experience U.S. tariffs in the same way as a result of the different priorities of governments. Germany possesses disproportionate influence within the EU because of its large economy, strong industrial base, and central role in European export networks. Consequently, it tends to prioritise market access, supply-chain stability, and the competitiveness of its manufacturers. France, by contrast, exercises influence through its diplomatic weight, military capabilities, and long-standing support for strategic autonomy, leading it to favour stronger industrial policy and state intervention. Poland has also become increasingly influential because of its population size, regional position, and growing security importance, but it continues to rely heavily on EU coordination and financing. These differences mean that the same tariff shock creates different political reactions inside the EU. As a result, member states may struggle to agree on a common response, reducing the EU's ability to act cohesively in trade negotiations and economic policymaking. This weakens the Union's collective bargaining power, creates policy uncertainty, and can limit the effectiveness of measures designed to protect European industries. Consequently, U.S. tariffs not only impose direct economic costs but also risk undermining economic coordination and political unity within the EU.

This divergence affects the process of cooperation. Countries that are highly export-dependent want quick and predictable trade deals. Countries that prefer more state intervention want the EU to use tariffs, subsidies, or industrial policy to defend its own markets. Countries that fear losing sovereignty are less willing to support deeper centralization. As a result, even when all member states agree that the U.S. tariff turn is a problem, they do not agree on the same solution. That is why unity in the EU is usually selective. The Union can often agree on broad goals, such as resilience or strategic autonomy, but it struggles when it has to choose specific goals.

## What Collaboration Has Already Happened

Recent events show that collaboration within the EU is no longer just an idea; it is becoming concrete. One of the clearest examples is joint defence procurement, where the EU has expanded instruments such as SAFE, EDIRPA, and EDIP to encourage member states to buy military equipment together rather than separately as a form of increasing collaboration towards collective saftey. This matters because the European Defence Agency has long found that collaborative defence spending remained far below EU targets, with only 18% of defence investment taking place collaboratively in 2022, even though the benchmark had been 35% for years. The first wave of SAFE funding, approved for eight member states, shows that these tools are now producing actual financial commitments. (European Commission, 26 Jan. 2026)

Another major case is the France-Germany joint declaration in 2026, which created a high-level nuclear steering group and committed both states to deeper cooperation on deterrence, early warning, air defence, and deep precision strike. The agreement is particularly significant because Germany's economic leadership and France's military and diplomatic influence often shape the direction of wider European integration. Therefore, their cooperation can influence the direction of European security policy more broadly. The EU has also used joint procurement in other crises, including COVID-19 vaccines, gas supplies, and ammunition for Ukraine, showing that crisis pressure can turn shared vulnerability into shared action. (European Parliamentary Research Service, 2026)

In energy policy, however, the pattern is more uneven: some states have embraced diversification and energy-community targets more fully than others, which shows that EU cooperation often advances through a common framework but uneven national implementation (European Parliamentary Research Service, 2026). Together, these examples show that the EU is already cooperating more in response to external pressure, but the cooperation is selective, uneven, and concentrated in sectors where states see direct strategic value.

## Long-Term Effects on Cooperation

In the long run, reciprocal tariffs may weaken the American-led world order by making the U.S. look less reliable as the anchor of open trade. If partners believe the U.S. may use tariffs again, they will plan for a future with more uncertainty and more fragmentation. That does not mean the U.S. loses all power, but it does mean that its leadership becomes less trusted. For Europe, that creates an incentive to rely more on itself and less on external stability. Over time, this could lead to a more coordinated EU economy, more defence integration, and more efforts to keep production, finance, and investment inside Europe.

But the long-term outcome is not guaranteed. The EU's deeper collaboration depends on whether member states can accept more shared responsibility. Germany may want competitive openness, France may want strategic protection, Ireland may want market access, and Poland may want security guarantees and flexibility. If those priorities clash too much, the EU may only achieve partial cooperation. If they align, the Union could build stronger resilience and more economic independence. That is why the real outcome of the tariff shock is not full detachment from America, but a slow shift toward a more self-reliant and more internally coordinated Europe (Reuters, 17 Apr. 2026).

## Conclusion

The EU cannot fully detach from the American-led economy in the short term, because the U.S. is still a major export market and a central part of Europe's trade system. At the same time, U.S. reciprocal tariffs are pushing Europe to rethink dependence, strengthen internal collaboration, and build self-reliance in strategic sectors. The main limits are economic differences between member states, political disagreement, and the EU's slow decision-making system. But the main result is clear: tariffs are making Europe more aware that cooperation inside the Union is not just a political ideal, but a practical need.
